What your home’s age means

What El Dorado Hills’s homes mean for your plumbing

The age of a house tells you more about its plumbing than almost anything else. A home built before 1960, one built in the 1970s, and one built last year have different pipes, different failure points and different jobs waiting for them — and in El Dorado Hills all three sit on the same streets. This is what the age of a home in El Dorado Hills usually means for the plumbing inside it.

Built before 1960: galvanised steel and cast iron

Homes built before about 1960 usually have galvanised steel supply pipes and cast-iron drains. Galvanised steel rusts from the inside, so the bore narrows over decades: pressure falls, water runs brown after a holiday away, and eventually a pinhole leak appears. Cast-iron drains scale and sag in the same way. A home of this age in El Dorado Hills is worth a proper look at the pipework, even if nothing has failed yet — the first signs are easy to miss.

1960s to 1980s: copper supply, plastic drains

Copper and plastic define the 1960s to 1980s: copper supply lines, ABS or PVC drains. It was a good era for plumbing, and most of it is still sound — but copper can develop pinhole leaks where the water chemistry is aggressive, and the drains are now old enough for joints and fittings to fail. The checks here are targeted: pressure, visible joints, and the drains that serve the busiest bathrooms.

1990s to today: PEX and CPVC

Homes built from the 1990s onward usually have PEX or CPVC supply lines — flexible, resistant to freezing and scale, and quick to install. They are not trouble-free: fittings matter, ultraviolet light degrades exposed runs, and a bad manifold installation causes pressure problems. The checks here are different — fittings, manifolds and the water heater — rather than the pipes themselves.

In the mountains, the era matters less than the exposure: a pipe in an unheated garage or crawl space will freeze whatever it is made of, and freeze-thaw cycles work joints loose over a winter. Insulating exposed runs and shutting off exterior taps before the first cold snap matters more here than any pipe material.

Common plumbing problems in El Dorado Hills, in depth

The four problems above are the ones we see most often in El Dorado Hills. Below is how to stop each of them coming back, plus two more faults that the Sierra Nevada and mountain towns conditions around El Dorado Hills produce regularly, covered in full.

01

Preventing clogged drains due to tree roots

How to prevent it: If trees stand near the line, have it camera-checked every year or two and the roots cleared before they mat into a full blockage — a drain that slows a little more each month is the warning.

02

Preventing leaky faucets in older homes

How to prevent it: Replace washers and cartridges at the first drip rather than living with it — a tap that drips all day wastes a surprising amount of water over a year, and the fix is a small part, not a new tap.

03

Preventing water heater failures during winter

How to prevent it: Listen to the heater when it fires: rumbling or popping means scale is building, and a flush now is cheaper than a replacement later.

04

Preventing running toilets wasting water

How to prevent it: Check the toilet once a year — the flapper, the fill valve, the seal at the base. A rocking toilet is a failed wax ring quietly rotting the floor underneath.

05

Water heater strain in winter

Why it happens: Cold inlet water in winter means the heater works harder and longer to reach temperature, which exposes a scaled tank or a failing thermostat that coped fine in summer.

How we fix it: We flush the tank, test the thermostat and the anode rod, and check the venting — a heater that cannot keep up in January usually needs a flush, not a replacement.

How to prevent it: Flush the tank in the autumn, before the cold inlet water arrives. If the hot water runs short only in winter, that is a capacity problem, not a broken heater.

06

Cracked toilet cisterns in the cold

Why it happens: Porcelain cisterns can crack in a hard freeze, especially in a bathroom that is left cold — the crack usually appears at the thaw, as a slow leak at the base.
