What your home’s age means

What Bakersfield’s homes mean for your plumbing

The age of a house tells you more about its plumbing than almost anything else. A home built before 1960, one built in the 1970s, and one built last year have different pipes, different failure points and different jobs waiting for them — and in Bakersfield all three sit on the same streets. This is what the age of a home in Bakersfield usually means for the plumbing inside it.

Built before 1960: galvanised steel and cast iron

A pre-1960 home in Bakersfield usually runs on galvanised steel supply pipes and cast-iron drains, and both wear from the inside out. The supply lines rust and narrow, so pressure falls and water runs brown after the house has stood empty; the drains scale and sag, so they block more often and clear more slowly. The work on a home of this age is usually staged: test the lines, replace the worst run first, and plan the rest over a year or two rather than all at once.

1960s to 1980s: copper supply, plastic drains

The 1960s to 1980s brought copper supply lines and plastic drains, and most of that work is still sound in Bakersfield. Copper's weakness is pinhole leaks where the water chemistry is aggressive, and the drains' weakness is age — joints and fittings from this era are now old enough to fail. The checks are targeted: pressure, visible joints, and the busiest bathrooms first.

1990s to today: PEX and CPVC

Homes built from the 1990s onward usually have PEX or CPVC supply lines — flexible, resistant to freezing and scale, and quick to install. They are not trouble-free: fittings matter, ultraviolet light degrades exposed runs, and a bad manifold installation causes pressure problems. The checks here are different — fittings, manifolds and the water heater — rather than the pipes themselves.

In the Valley, hard water is the thread running through all three eras: it scales galvanised lines, clogs copper fittings and settles in PEX manifolds alike. Whatever the pipe material, a water heater that is flushed annually and an anode rod that is replaced on schedule will outlast one that is never serviced.

Why it happens: Many California homes sit on concrete slabs, and a pinhole leak in a buried hot-water line shows up as a warm patch on the floor, a faint sound of running water, or a water bill that climbs for no reason.

How we fix it: We locate the leak with acoustic and electronic detection, then reroute the line overhead or make a spot repair — whichever does less damage to the home.

How to prevent it: Know your normal water bill. A sudden rise with no change in use is the earliest sign of a slab leak, and catching it early is the difference between a reroute and a ruined floor.

06

Sewer odours from dried traps

Why it happens: Traps dry out in unused bathrooms and guest rooms, and heat speeds it up — once the water seal is gone, sewer smells come straight up the drain.

How we fix it: We run water through every trap, check the venting, and find any trap that is not holding its seal. A trap that keeps drying out usually has a venting fault behind it.

How to prevent it: Run water in every sink, shower and bath at least once a month, including the ones nobody uses. A trap that stays full is a trap that keeps smells out.

Plumbing needs in Bakersfield change with the seasons. Here is what to expect through the year, and the small checks that prevent the usual seasonal faults.

Spring

Spring is the season to undo winter’s wear: clear the gutters, check the drains, and look over any pipework that spent the winter cold and damp.

Tip: Schedule a plumbing inspection to ensure your drainage systems are clear and functioning properly.

Summer

Summer heat can cause pipes to expand, leading to leaks and pressure issues.

Tip: Keep an eye on your water bill for unexpected increases, which may indicate leaks that need addressing.

Fall

Fall is a time for preparing plumbing for winter, including checking for leaks and insulating pipes.

Tip: Consider scheduling a maintenance check to ensure your plumbing is ready for cooler temperatures.

Winter

Winter is when small problems become emergencies: a cold snap freezes an exposed pipe, a scaled heater gives up under the extra load, and a blocked drain backs up in the first storm.

Tip: Before the cold arrives, insulate exposed pipes, clear the gutters, and have the water heater checked — the three jobs that prevent most winter call-outs.

Water and weather in Bakersfield

Water and weather in Bakersfield: what it does to plumbing

Plumbing fails for local reasons. In Bakersfield the local reasons are hard water and long, hot summers, plus very hot, dry summers and cool, foggy winters — and understanding them is the difference between a repair that lasts and one that comes back next season.

Hard water is the Valley's slow tax on plumbing. Every litre leaves a little mineral behind, and over years that mineral narrows pipes, clogs aerators and coats the inside of the water heater until it heats half as well for twice the running cost. The visible sign is white scale on taps and showerheads; the expensive sign is a heater that rumbles, runs out of hot water early, or fails years before its time. Flushing the tank once a year is the single most valuable maintenance job in Bakersfield.

Summer heat works on the outside of the system. Exposed supply lines expand and contract every day, hoses and irrigation connections bake in the sun, and plastic fittings go brittle. The failures cluster in August: split joints, cracked hose connections, a damp patch by the house wall that nobody noticed for weeks. Shading or lagging the exposed runs and checking them before the heat peaks is the Valley version of winterising — and it takes an hour.

Dust is the Valley's quiet plumbing problem. Dry-season dust and field debris collect in outdoor drains and gutters, then wash into a solid blockage with the first autumn rain. A clear-out before the first rain is the cheapest drainage job of the year — and the same dust clogs irrigation filters and outdoor tap aerators, so a weak outdoor tap in September is usually a filter, not a supply fault.

Local problem patterns

Plumbing problems common in the Central Valley

Hard water and summer heat write the plumbing story in the Valley. Around Bakersfield, the same five faults appear year after year.

  • Ageing galvanised supply lines — older Valley homes often still run on steel pipe that corrodes internally and throttles pressure.
  • High summer demand — garden watering and long showers push older systems hard, and weak points tend to fail in July and August.
  • Water heater sediment — mineral sediment settles at the bottom of the tank, making the heater work harder, sound louder and die younger.
  • Furred-up taps and showerheads — white scale on fixtures is the visible half of the problem; the same scale is narrowing the pipes behind the wall.
  • Split outdoor fittings — heat expansion and UV wear crack exposed plastic and rubber fittings, usually discovered as a puddle by the house wall.

Most Valley plumbing trouble is mineral trouble. A yearly heater flush and a look at the fixtures buys years of quiet service.
