What your home’s age means

What Concord’s homes mean for your plumbing

Two houses on the same street in Concord can have completely different plumbing, because the year they were built decides what is in the walls. Knowing what era a home belongs to tells you what to check, what is likely to fail first, and whether a repair or a replacement is the honest answer.

Built before 1960: galvanised steel and cast iron

A pre-1960 home in Concord usually runs on galvanised steel supply pipes and cast-iron drains, and both wear from the inside out. The supply lines rust and narrow, so pressure falls and water runs brown after the house has stood empty; the drains scale and sag, so they block more often and clear more slowly. The work on a home of this age is usually staged: test the lines, replace the worst run first, and plan the rest over a year or two rather than all at once.

1960s to 1980s: copper supply, plastic drains

The 1960s to 1980s brought copper supply lines and plastic drains, and most of that work is still sound in Concord. Copper's weakness is pinhole leaks where the water chemistry is aggressive, and the drains' weakness is age — joints and fittings from this era are now old enough to fail. The checks are targeted: pressure, visible joints, and the busiest bathrooms first.

1990s to today: PEX and CPVC

Homes built from the 1990s onward usually have PEX or CPVC supply lines — flexible, resistant to freezing and scale, and quick to install. They are not trouble-free: fittings matter, ultraviolet light degrades exposed runs, and a bad manifold installation causes pressure problems. The checks here are different — fittings, manifolds and the water heater — rather than the pipes themselves.

In the Bay Area, the ground is the factor no era escapes: clay soils swell and shrink with the seasons, working joints open and bellying buried laterals. Whatever the supply pipes are made of, a camera inspection of the lateral is worth doing on any older home here.

Water and weather in Concord

Water and weather in Concord: what it does to plumbing

Plumbing fails for local reasons. In Concord the local reasons are older housing stock, clay soils and damp fog, plus cool, foggy summers and wet winters — and understanding them is the difference between a repair that lasts and one that comes back next season.

Age is the Bay Area's plumbing story. Many homes in Concord were built before the 1960s, and their pipes have been wearing ever since: galvanised supply lines narrowing with rust, cast-iron drains scaling and sagging. The signs creep up slowly — a shower that has lost pressure over the years, water that runs brown after a holiday — and they are easy to live with until they are not. A pressure and flow test tells you in one visit how much life the lines have left.

The ground here moves with the seasons. Expansive clay soils swell in the wet winter and shrink in the dry summer, and that movement works on everything buried: sewer laterals belly and joints open, water lines shift, and tree roots find the gaps. A camera inspection of the lateral is the only way to know what is happening underground — and it is worth doing before buying an older home in Concord, or after any backup that clears and then returns.

Water pressure tells the story of a Bay Area home. If the shower has slowly lost pressure over the years, the cause is usually inside the pipes: galvanised lines narrowing with rust, or scale building where the water is hard. A pressure test at several points separates a supply problem from a pipework problem in minutes, and the fix — descaling a run, or replacing the worst section — is usually smaller than people fear.

Local problem patterns

Plumbing problems common in the Bay Area

Bay Area plumbing has a character of its own: old pipes, hills, big trees and earthquakes. These are the problems that come up again and again in Concord.

  • Water pressure that varies by floor — hillside and multi-storey homes often see weak pressure upstairs, where long runs and old pipes struggle to deliver.
  • Old water heaters in small spaces — Bay Area utility closets and garages often hold ageing heaters that fail without warning, and access for replacement is tight.
  • Ageing galvanised pipes — many homes still have original steel supply lines that corrode from the inside, cutting pressure and tinting the water brown.
  • Tree roots in sewer laterals — mature street trees send roots into old clay and cast-iron joints, and the first sign is slow drains in several rooms at once.
  • Hillside drain runs — long laterals on sloped lots are harder to clear and more prone to sagging sections where waste collects.

Start with the age of the pipes and the state of the sewer line — those two checks tell you most of what you need to know.
