What your home’s age means

What Fort Bragg’s homes mean for your plumbing

Two houses on the same street in Fort Bragg can have completely different plumbing, because the year they were built decides what is in the walls. Knowing what era a home belongs to tells you what to check, what is likely to fail first, and whether a repair or a replacement is the honest answer.

Built before 1960: galvanised steel and cast iron

Before 1960, the standard was galvanised steel for supply and cast iron for drains, and both are now past their best in Fort Bragg. The steel rusts from the inside, narrowing the bore until pressure falls and the water runs brown; the cast iron scales and sags until drains block more often. Test the lines before anything fails — a pressure and flow test, plus a camera look at the drains, tells you what is urgent and what can wait.

1960s to 1980s: copper supply, plastic drains

Homes from the 1960s to the 1980s usually have copper supply lines and ABS or PVC drains — a big step up. Copper lasts well, but it can develop pinhole leaks where the water is aggressive or the pipe was poorly installed, and the drains from this era are now reaching the age where joints and fittings start to fail. The work here is usually targeted: replace the worst run, not the whole system.

1990s to today: PEX and CPVC

Homes built from the 1990s onward usually have PEX or CPVC supply lines — flexible, resistant to freezing and scale, and quick to install. They are not trouble-free: fittings matter, ultraviolet light degrades exposed runs, and a bad manifold installation causes pressure problems. The checks here are different — fittings, manifolds and the water heater — rather than the pipes themselves.

On the North Coast, damp is the thread through every era: months of wet weather keep crawl spaces moist and corrode exposed pipework, whatever it is made of. Ventilation under the house, clear gutters and lagged cold-water lines matter as much here as the pipe material in the walls.

Common plumbing problems in Fort Bragg, in depth

The four problems above are the ones we see most often in Fort Bragg. Below is what prevents each of them, plus two more faults that the North Coast conditions around Fort Bragg produce regularly, covered in full.

01

Frequent leaks in older homes

How to prevent it: Look for the early signs — a drip that will not stop, a stain on the wall, a warm patch on the floor — and act on them. A leak caught early is a washer; the same leak ignored for a season is a ruined cabinet.

02

Clogs due to coastal debris

How to prevent it: Find the main shut-off valve before you need it, and make sure everyone in the house knows where it is. In a leak, the first minute matters more than the first hour.

03

Water heater failures during peak demand

How to prevent it: Flush the tank once a year and replace the anode rod on schedule. If the hot water runs out faster than it used to, book the flush before the heater gives up entirely.

04

Sump pump failures during heavy rains

How to prevent it: If the house has a sump pump, pour water into the pit twice a year and watch it work. A pump that is never tested is a pump that fails on the one night it is needed.

05

Seized shut-off valves

Why it happens: Stopcocks and shut-off valves seize from disuse and corrosion, and the failure only shows up in an emergency, when the water cannot be turned off.

How we fix it: We free the valve where possible and replace it with a lever valve where it has seized solid, so the water can be shut off in seconds.

How to prevent it: Work every shut-off valve in the house twice a year — kitchen, bathroom, water heater, and the main. A valve that moves freely is the cheapest insurance in plumbing.

06

Noisy pipes and water hammer

Why it happens: A bang when a tap shuts, or a rattle when the washing machine fills, usually means loose clips, high pressure, or air in the lines — and it gets worse, not better, if it is ignored.

How we fix it: We secure the loose runs, check the pressure, and fit a water hammer arrestor where the bang is worst. The fix is usually small; the damage from ignoring it is not.

How to prevent it: Note when the noise started and which tap causes it — that history tells the technician where to look, and whether pressure or a loose clip is the cause.

Water and weather in Fort Bragg: what it does to plumbing

Rain is the North Coast's plumbing season. The North Coast's older homes carry their age in the pipes. Timber houses here often still run on galvanised supply lines and cast-iron drains, and the signs are the usual ones: falling pressure, water that runs brown after a holiday away, a drain that slows every winter. A pressure and flow test plus a camera look at the lateral will tell you in one visit whether the system has years left or needs staged replacement.

Roots are the North Coast's sewer problem. Mature trees and older clay or cast-iron laterals are a combination that ends in root intrusion, and a drain that slows every winter is usually telling you the roots have found a joint. A camera inspection shows exactly where they are and whether a cut, a lining or a replacement is the right fix.
