What your home’s age means

What Clovis’s homes mean for your plumbing

Two houses on the same street in Clovis can have completely different plumbing, because the year they were built decides what is in the walls. Knowing what era a home belongs to tells you what to check, what is likely to fail first, and whether a repair or a replacement is the honest answer.

Built before 1960: galvanised steel and cast iron

Before 1960, the standard was galvanised steel for supply and cast iron for drains, and both are now past their best in Clovis. The steel rusts from the inside, narrowing the bore until pressure falls and the water runs brown; the cast iron scales and sags until drains block more often. Test the lines before anything fails — a pressure and flow test, plus a camera look at the drains, tells you what is urgent and what can wait.

1960s to 1980s: copper supply, plastic drains

Homes from the 1960s to the 1980s usually have copper supply lines and ABS or PVC drains — a big step up. Copper lasts well, but it can develop pinhole leaks where the water is aggressive or the pipe was poorly installed, and the drains from this era are now reaching the age where joints and fittings start to fail. The work here is usually targeted: replace the worst run, not the whole system.

1990s to today: PEX and CPVC

From the 1990s onward, PEX and CPVC took over: flexible, freeze-resistant, quick to install. The failure points moved to the fittings and the manifold, and to exposed runs left in the sun. A home of this era in Clovis rarely needs repiping — it needs its fittings, its manifold and its water heater checked, which is a smaller job and a shorter visit.

In the Valley, hard water is the thread through every era: it scales galvanised lines, clogs copper fittings and settles in PEX manifolds alike. Whatever the pipe material, the habits are the same — flush the heater annually, replace the anode rod on schedule, and descale the aerators when the flow drops.

Water and weather in Clovis

Water and weather in Clovis: what it does to plumbing

Plumbing fails for local reasons. In Clovis the local reasons are hard water and long, hot summers, plus very hot, dry summers and cool, foggy winters — and understanding them is the difference between a repair that lasts and one that comes back next season.

Hard water is the Valley's slow tax on plumbing. Every litre leaves a little mineral behind, and over years that mineral narrows pipes, clogs aerators and coats the inside of the water heater until it heats half as well for twice the running cost. The visible sign is white scale on taps and showerheads; the expensive sign is a heater that rumbles, runs out of hot water early, or fails years before its time. Flushing the tank once a year is the single most valuable maintenance job in Clovis.

Summer heat works on the outside of the system. Exposed supply lines expand and contract every day, hoses and irrigation connections bake in the sun, and plastic fittings go brittle. The failures cluster in August: split joints, cracked hose connections, a damp patch by the house wall that nobody noticed for weeks. Shading or lagging the exposed runs and checking them before the heat peaks is the Valley version of winterising — and it takes an hour.

Dust is the Valley's quiet plumbing problem. Dry-season dust and field debris collect in outdoor drains and gutters, then wash into a solid blockage with the first autumn rain. A clear-out before the first rain is the cheapest drainage job of the year — and the same dust clogs irrigation filters and outdoor tap aerators, so a weak outdoor tap in September is usually a filter, not a supply fault.
