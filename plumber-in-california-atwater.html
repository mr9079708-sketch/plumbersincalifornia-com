What your home’s age means

What Atwater’s homes mean for your plumbing

Two houses on the same street in Atwater can have completely different plumbing, because the year they were built decides what is in the walls. Knowing what era a home belongs to tells you what to check, what is likely to fail first, and whether a repair or a replacement is the honest answer.

Built before 1960: galvanised steel and cast iron

Homes built before about 1960 usually have galvanised steel supply pipes and cast-iron drains. Galvanised steel rusts from the inside, so the bore narrows over decades: pressure falls, water runs brown after a holiday away, and eventually a pinhole leak appears. Cast-iron drains scale and sag in the same way. A home of this age in Atwater is worth a proper look at the pipework, even if nothing has failed yet — the first signs are easy to miss.

1960s to 1980s: copper supply, plastic drains

The 1960s to 1980s brought copper supply lines and plastic drains, and most of that work is still sound in Atwater. Copper's weakness is pinhole leaks where the water chemistry is aggressive, and the drains' weakness is age — joints and fittings from this era are now old enough to fail. The checks are targeted: pressure, visible joints, and the busiest bathrooms first.

1990s to today: PEX and CPVC

From the 1990s onward, PEX and CPVC took over: flexible, freeze-resistant, quick to install. The failure points moved to the fittings and the manifold, and to exposed runs left in the sun. A home of this era in Atwater rarely needs repiping — it needs its fittings, its manifold and its water heater checked, which is a smaller job and a shorter visit.

In the Valley, hard water is the thread through every era: it scales galvanised lines, clogs copper fittings and settles in PEX manifolds alike. Whatever the pipe material, the habits are the same — flush the heater annually, replace the anode rod on schedule, and descale the aerators when the flow drops.

Flushing the tank once a year and replacing the anode rod on schedule is the cheapest maintenance in plumbing, and in Atwater it is the maintenance that matters most.

Summer heat works on the outside of the system. Exposed supply lines expand and contract every day, hoses and irrigation connections bake in the sun, and plastic fittings go brittle. The failures cluster in August: split joints, cracked hose connections, a damp patch by the house wall that nobody noticed for weeks. Shading or lagging the exposed runs and checking them before the heat peaks is the Valley version of winterising — and it takes an hour.

Drought shapes fixtures here too. Low-flow toilets, aerators and showerheads are standard in newer Valley homes, and they save real water — but they are less forgiving of scale and partial blockages, so a slow flush or a weak shower in Atwater is often a scale problem rather than a pressure problem.
