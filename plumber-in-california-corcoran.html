What your home’s age means

What Corcoran’s homes mean for your plumbing

The age of a house tells you more about its plumbing than almost anything else. A home built before 1960, one built in the 1970s, and one built last year have different pipes, different failure points and different jobs waiting for them — and in Corcoran all three sit on the same streets. This is what the age of a home in Corcoran usually means for the plumbing inside it.

Built before 1960: galvanised steel and cast iron

A pre-1960 home in Corcoran usually runs on galvanised steel supply pipes and cast-iron drains, and both wear from the inside out. The supply lines rust and narrow, so pressure falls and water runs brown after the house has stood empty; the drains scale and sag, so they block more often and clear more slowly. The work on a home of this age is usually staged: test the lines, replace the worst run first, and plan the rest over a year or two rather than all at once.

1960s to 1980s: copper supply, plastic drains

Copper and plastic define the 1960s to 1980s: copper supply lines, ABS or PVC drains. It was a good era for plumbing, and most of it is still sound — but copper can develop pinhole leaks where the water chemistry is aggressive, and the drains are now old enough for joints and fittings to fail. The checks here are targeted: pressure, visible joints, and the drains that serve the busiest bathrooms.

1990s to today: PEX and CPVC

PEX and CPVC define homes built from the 1990s onward: flexible pipe that resists freezing and scale, and installs quickly. The weak points moved, they did not vanish — fittings, manifolds and the quality of the installation now decide whether the system is trouble-free. Exposed runs still need protection from sun, and a bad manifold will show up as pressure problems in one part of the house and not another.

In the Valley, hard water is the thread running through all three eras: it scales galvanised lines, clogs copper fittings and settles in PEX manifolds alike. Whatever the pipe material, a water heater that is flushed annually and an anode rod that is replaced on schedule will outlast one that is never serviced.

Water and weather in Corcoran: what it does to plumbing

Water is the defining plumbing factor in Corcoran. Supply across the Central Valley is hard, and mineral scale is the result: it narrows pipes, clogs aerators and showerheads, and settles inside water heaters, where it insulates the burner or element from the water. A heater full of scale works harder, costs more to run and dies years early. Flushing the tank once a year and replacing the anode rod on schedule is the cheapest maintenance in plumbing, and in Kings County it is the maintenance that matters most.

Heat is the second factor. Long, hot summers expand exposed supply lines and bake hoses, irrigation connections and the plastic fittings on outdoor equipment. Lines that sit in direct sun expand and contract every day, which works joints loose over a season; shading or lagging exposed runs, and checking them before the heat peaks, prevents the split joints and weeping connections we see every August. Dust and field debris also find their way into outdoor drains and gutters during the dry season, so a clear-out before the first autumn rain is good practice.

Drought shapes fixtures here too. Low-flow toilets, aerators and showerheads are standard in newer Valley homes, and they save real water — but they are less forgiving of scale and partial blockages, so a slow flush or a weak shower in Corcoran is often a scale problem rather than a pressure problem. A descaling clean of the aerators and a check of the fill valve usually restores normal flow without replacing anything.

Local problem patterns

Plumbing problems common in the Central Valley

The Central Valley is tough on plumbing in two ways: the water is hard and the summers are long. Here is what that means in Corcoran.
