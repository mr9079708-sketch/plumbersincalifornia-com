Know your home

Housing in Long Beach: what your pipes are made of — and why it matters

A plumber who does not know what your pipes are made of is guessing. In Long Beach, as across the region, the material depends mostly on when the house was built, and each era has its own failure pattern. This is what we see, era by era.

Before the 1960s: galvanised steel and cast iron

The oldest homes in Long Beach usually have galvanised steel supply pipes and cast-iron drains. Galvanised steel corrodes from the inside, so the bore narrows year by year: pressure falls, water runs rust-coloured after a holiday, and eventually a joint weeps or a section blocks. Cast-iron drains scale and crack in the same way, and they are the pipes tree roots find first. A repair buys time; a house where the pressure has dropped in every tap is usually ready for a planned repipe in copper or PEX rather than another patch.

1960s to 1980s: copper supply, plastic drains

From the 1960s on, Long Beach homes usually have copper supply lines and ABS or PVC drains. Copper is a long-lived material, but aggressive water can open pinhole leaks and soldered joints age with the house. The drains are generally sound — the weak point is the sewer lateral, the run to the street, which is often clay or cast iron even in a copper house, and that is where roots get in. A camera inspection answers the question in an hour.

1990s to 2000s: copper and early PEX

In 1990s and 2000s homes in Long Beach, expect copper and early PEX with PVC drains. The PEX itself is durable and scale-resistant, but the fittings and manifolds from that period should be looked at, and water heaters installed then are now nearing replacement age — worth checking before they fail rather than after.

2010s onwards: PEX and CPVC

Newer homes in Long Beach are mostly PEX or CPVC, often run from a central manifold so each fixture can be isolated on its own. The pipe itself rarely fails; when something does go wrong it is usually a fitting, a valve or an appliance connection. Tankless water heaters are common in this era too, and they need descaling on a schedule — in hard-water country, skipping it is the fastest way to a cold shower.

How to tell what you have: look at an exposed pipe near the water heater or under a sink. Galvanised steel is dull grey, magnetic and joined with threads; copper is reddish-brown with soldered joints; PEX is flexible plastic, often red for hot and blue for cold; CPVC is rigid cream-coloured plastic.

What we check on a visit in Long Beach: the pipe material and its condition, water pressure at more than one tap, the water heater's age and anode, and every visible joint for weeping. The report is plain: what is fine, what needs watching, and what should be planned before it fails.

Local conditions

Water and climate in Long Beach: what local conditions do to your plumbing

Salt-laden sea air is the defining force on plumbing here. It corrodes exposed brass and copper fittings, outdoor taps and water-heater connections faster than inland air does, and the damp fog that rolls in off the water keeps crawl spaces and under-sink cupboards moist, so a slow leak can run unnoticed for months.

Water supplies along the coast are generally moderately hard. That is enough to leave scale in kettles and showerheads over a few years, and to shorten a water heater's life if it is never flushed, though it is less aggressive than the groundwater of the Central Valley.

What that means for a homeowner in Long Beach is simple: the local conditions set the maintenance schedule. A heater flush once a year, a look at exposed fittings each spring, and a check of outdoor lines before the season that stresses them most — those three habits prevent most of the expensive calls we get.

Local conditions change what goes wrong and how often. These notes describe the area around Long Beach in general terms, so you know what to watch for.

  • This area is in Los Angeles County.
  • Salt air can speed up corrosion on exposed pipes, fittings and outdoor fixtures, so it is worth checking them for rust and wear.
  • Damp sea air and occasional heavy rain can keep crawl spaces and outdoor plumbing damp; keep drainage and gutters clear around the home.
  • Older homes near the coast often have ageing supply lines; a periodic check for small leaks helps catch problems early.
  • Salt-laden air attacks unprotected metal, so pipe clips, brackets and outdoor fixtures deserve a look at least once a year.
  • Low-lying streets near the shore can see standing water after heavy rain; make sure surface drains run away from the house, not towards it.

Wherever you are in California, the basics are the same — find the main water shut-off before you need it, consider a seismic shut-off valve, and do not let small leaks run.

We check exposed metalwork on every visit, and replacing a corroded fitting early is far cheaper than repairing the water damage it causes later.

A standard tank water heater typically lasts 8 to 12 years, and in Long Beach the local water is the deciding factor: scale shortens its life, so a heater that is flushed every year usually outlasts one that never is. Local problem patterns

Plumbing problems common on the California coast

Local conditions write the plumbing story in Long Beach. Around here, the same five faults appear year after year — the list below, and the detail on each one further down the page.

  • Outdoor showers and pool equipment — coastal homes often have outdoor showers and pool plumbing, and their exposed fittings corrode faster than indoor ones.
  • Irrigation and garden lines — watering systems run all year near the coast, and a cracked line or backflow fault can waste water for weeks before anyone notices.
  • Corroded outdoor taps and fittings — salt air eats unprotected metal, so exterior pipework, hose bibs and clips rust faster than the same parts inland.
  • Sand and grit in outdoor drains — wind-blown sand settles in gullies and yard drains, slowing them until a heavy rain backs water up.
  • Damp crawl spaces — sea air keeps the space under the house moist, which corrodes fittings and encourages mould where leaks go unnoticed.

Check the outdoor pipework once a year and clear the drains after storms — that alone prevents most coastal plumbing emergencies.
