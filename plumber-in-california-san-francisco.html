Know your home

Housing in San Francisco: what your pipes are made of — and why it matters

Ask a plumber in San Francisco what matters most on a first visit and the answer is usually the same: the age of the house and the material of its pipes. The two are linked, and knowing the link tells you what is likely to fail next.

Before the 1940s: galvanised steel and cast iron

San Francisco's oldest homes — the Victorians and Edwardians that line the hills — were plumbed in galvanised steel for supply and cast iron for drains, and plenty of that pipework is still in service. Galvanised steel corrodes from the inside: the bore narrows a little every year, pressure drops, the water runs rust-coloured after a holiday away, and sooner or later a joint weeps or a section blocks solid. Cast-iron drains age the same way, scaling and cracking until tree roots find their way in. A repair can buy time, but when pressure has fallen at every tap, the honest answer is usually a planned repipe in copper or PEX.

1940s to 1970s: copper arrives, clay laterals stay

Homes from the 1940s, 50s, 60s and 70s in San Francisco are usually copper for supply, but the sewer lateral to the street is often still clay or cast iron, and roots exploit every joint. Copper lasts well, though pinhole leaks from aggressive water and tired soldered joints are not unusual. In these homes the water heater is often tucked into a small garage or closet, so access matters as much as the unit itself. A camera survey of the lateral settles the question in one visit.

1980s to 2000s: copper and early PEX

The 1980s, 90s and 2000s brought the first PEX alongside a lot more copper, with PVC drains throughout. PEX from this period is flexible and resists scale, but the early fittings and manifolds deserve a look: a manifold weeping slowly behind a panel is easy to miss until the damp shows. Water heaters from this era are now reaching the end of their working life, so a heater check is worth doing even while the hot water still runs.

2010s onwards: PEX and CPVC

Homes built since the 2010s in San Francisco are mostly PEX or CPVC, often run from a central manifold that lets each fixture be isolated on its own. The pipe itself rarely fails; trouble usually starts at a fitting, a valve or an appliance connection. Tankless heaters are common in these homes too, and they need descaling on schedule — skipping it is the fastest route to a cold shower.

How to tell what you have: look at an exposed pipe near the water heater or under a sink. Galvanised steel is dull grey, magnetic and joined with threads; copper is reddish-brown with soldered joints; PEX is flexible plastic, often red for hot and blue for cold; CPVC is rigid cream-coloured plastic.

What we check on a visit in San Francisco: the pipe material and its condition, water pressure at more than one tap and on more than one floor, the water heater's age and anode, and every visible joint for weeping. The report is plain: what is fine, what needs watching, and what should be planned before it fails.

Local conditions

Water and climate in San Francisco: what local conditions do to your plumbing

Fog and damp set the terms for plumbing in San Francisco. The cool, moist air that rolls in off the bay keeps crawl spaces and cupboards damp for months, so a slow leak can run unnoticed until the damage is done — and the older the house, the more joints there are to weep.

San Francisco is earthquake country, and that shapes the plumbing too. Flexible gas and water connectors and a seismic shut-off valve are the standard precautions, and water heaters are strapped in place rather than left free-standing. Knowing where the main gas and water shut-offs are is worth more than any tool in the van: in an earthquake the first minutes matter, and a household that has never located its shut-offs loses them.

None of this is a reason to worry; it is a reason to maintain. In San Francisco the homes that cost their owners least are the ones where the heater is flushed yearly, the crawl space is checked for damp once a year, and a small leak is fixed while it is still small.

In depth

The problems San Francisco homes face — in depth

Every city has a short list of faults that come up again and again. For San Francisco it is the five below, and each one has a cause you can understand, a fix that works, and a habit that prevents the next one.

Low pressure at upstairs taps

Why it happens in San Francisco: long runs and narrow, scaled pipes struggle to feed upper floors, especially in hillside homes, so the shower upstairs runs weak while the kitchen tap is fine.

How we fix it — and how to prevent the next one: the visit starts with a pressure check at taps on more than one floor, which tells us whether the fault is the supply, the pipework or a single fixture. A scaled section can be replaced, but where the whole run is narrowed, the durable fix is a planned repipe in copper or PEX. Prevention is a pressure check once a year: a slow fall over two visits is the warning to plan the repipe before a joint fails.

Gas and water shut-offs nobody can find

Why it happens in San Francisco: in an earthquake the first minutes matter, yet many households have never located their shut-off valves — and in an older home the valve may be seized, buried behind stored boxes, or both.

How we fix it — and how to prevent the next one: we locate both shut-offs on the visit, test that they turn, and fit a seismic shut-off valve and flexible connectors where they are missing. The location is written on the job record so the household knows it afterwards. Prevention costs nothing: once a year, walk to the shut-offs and turn them — a valve that moves yearly will move in an emergency.

Slow leaks under the house

Why it happens in San Francisco: damp crawl spaces hide drips for months, and the first clue is often a musty smell or a higher water bill.

How we fix it — and how to prevent the next one: we find the leak with listening gear and a pressure test, then repair the failed section and test the line end to end — no guesswork, no unnecessary digging. Where the crawl space stays damp, we say so plainly and suggest the drainage or ventilation fix that fits. Prevention is a look under the house once a year, and a glance at the water meter when nothing is running: a meter that turns when every tap is off is a leak.

Old water heaters in small spaces

Why it happens in San Francisco: Bay Area utility closets and garages often hold ageing heaters that fail without warning, and access for replacement is tight.

How we fix it — and how to prevent the next one: the heater is tested first — thermostat, element, anode rod and pressure — so repair and replacement can be priced side by side in writing. The replacement is measured for the space before it is ordered, and the new unit is strapped and vented to code. Prevention is a yearly flush and an anode check: a heater that is looked after usually warns you before it fails, with rust-coloured water or a rumbling noise.

Tree roots in ageing sewer laterals

Why it happens in San Francisco: the city's many trees send roots into clay and cast-iron laterals, and the first sign is usually a drain that keeps blocking no matter how often it is cleared.

How we fix it — and how to prevent the next one: a camera survey finds the exact intrusion first, so the repair fits the fault: a section replaced, or a trenchless repair where the pipe allows it. Clearing alone is never sold as a cure when roots are the cause. Prevention is a camera check every couple of years on a lateral of this age, and keeping new planting away from the line.

Plumbing in San Francisco is shaped by local conditions. The notes below are general to the area — worth knowing before you book, and useful when talking to the technician.

  • This area is in San Francisco County.
  • Many older homes still have galvanised or ageing pipes that narrow and corrode over time; low water pressure or discoloured water is a sign to have them checked.
  • Seismic shut-off valves and flexible connectors are common precautions in the area, and it is worth knowing where the main gas and water shut-offs are.
  • Cool, damp winters and fog can keep outdoor pipes and crawl spaces moist; watch for slow leaks and persistent damp.
  • Hillside homes can have long drain runs and older sewer laterals, and tree roots can work their way into ageing joints.
  • Earthquake country means flexible gas and water connectors and a shut-off valve are worth having — and worth knowing the location of before you need them.
  • Fog and cool damp winters keep crawl spaces moist for months; a yearly check for damp and slow leaks under the house pays for itself.

Wherever you are in California, the basics are the same — find the main water shut-off before you need it, consider a seismic shut-off valve, and do not let small leaks run.

Local problem patterns

Plumbing problems common in the Bay Area

The faults below are the ones San Francisco residents call about most. They follow from the local water, climate and housing, so they recur until the cause is dealt with — which is why each one gets a full treatment below.

  • Low pressure at upstairs taps — long runs and narrow, scaled pipes struggle to feed upper floors, especially in hillside homes.
  • Gas and water shut-offs nobody can find — in an earthquake the first minutes matter, yet many households have never located their shut-off valves.
  • Slow leaks under the house — damp crawl spaces hide drips for months, and the first clue is often a musty smell or a higher water bill.
  • Tree roots in ageing sewer laterals — the city’s many trees send roots into clay and cast-iron laterals, and the first sign is usually a drain that keeps blocking no matter how often it is cleared.
  • Old water heaters in small spaces — Bay Area utility closets and garages often hold ageing heaters that fail without warning, and access for replacement is tight.

A camera inspection of the sewer line and a look at the age of the supply pipes answers most Bay Area plumbing questions before they become emergencies.
