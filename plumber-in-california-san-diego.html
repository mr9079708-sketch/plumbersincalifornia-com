Know your home

Housing in San Diego: what your pipes are made of — and why it matters

Ask a plumber in San Diego what matters most on a first visit and the answer is usually the same: the age of the house and the material of its pipes. The two are linked, and knowing the link tells you what is likely to fail next.

Before the 1960s: galvanised steel and cast iron

San Diego's oldest homes — many of them in Hillcrest and along the older coastal streets — were plumbed in galvanised steel for supply and cast iron for drains, and plenty of that pipework is still in service. Galvanised steel corrodes from the inside: the bore narrows a little every year, pressure drops, the water runs rust-coloured after a holiday away, and sooner or later a joint weeps or a section blocks solid. Cast-iron drains age the same way, scaling and cracking until tree roots find their way in. A repair can buy time, but when pressure has fallen at every tap, the honest answer is usually a planned repipe in copper or PEX.

1960s to 1980s: copper supply, plastic drains

Homes from the 1960s, 70s and 80s in San Diego are usually copper for supply and plastic for drains. Copper lasts well here, though pinhole leaks from aggressive water and tired soldered joints are not unusual. The weak point is often outside the house: even where the supply is copper, the sewer lateral to the street is frequently clay or cast iron, and roots exploit every joint. A camera survey settles the question in one visit.

1990s to 2000s: copper and early PEX

The 1990s and 2000s brought the first PEX alongside a lot more copper, with PVC drains throughout. PEX from this period is flexible and resists scale, which suits San Diego's hard water, but the early fittings and manifolds deserve a look: a manifold weeping slowly behind a panel is easy to miss until the damp shows. Water heaters from this era are now reaching the end of their working life, so a heater check is worth doing even while the hot water still runs.

2010s onwards: PEX and CPVC

Homes built since the 2010s in San Diego are mostly PEX or CPVC, often run from a central manifold that lets each fixture be isolated on its own. The pipe itself rarely fails; trouble usually starts at a fitting, a valve or an appliance connection. Tankless heaters are common in these homes too, and they need descaling on schedule — with hard water, skipping it is the fastest route to a cold shower. Low-flow toilets and showerheads from the drought years are common as well, and they need the right parts when they fail.

How to tell what you have: look at an exposed pipe near the water heater or under a sink. Galvanised steel is dull grey, magnetic and joined with threads; copper is reddish-brown with soldered joints; PEX is flexible plastic, often red for hot and blue for cold; CPVC is rigid cream-coloured plastic.

What we check on a visit in San Diego: the pipe material and its condition, water pressure at more than one tap, the water heater's age and anode, and every visible joint for weeping. The report is plain: what is fine, what needs watching, and what should be planned before it fails.

Local conditions

Water and climate in San Diego: what local conditions do to your plumbing

The sea sets the terms for plumbing on the coast. Salt in the air works on every exposed fitting, and the damp air that rolls in off the Pacific keeps crawl spaces and cupboards moist, so a slow leak can run for months before anyone notices it.

The local water is hard in most districts. It leaves scale in kettles and showerheads over a few years and shortens a water heater's life if the heater is never flushed — a steady problem rather than a dramatic one, but it adds up. Drought-era fixtures are common here too: low-flow toilets and showerheads fitted during the dry years save water, and they need the right parts and the right pressure to work properly.

None of this is a reason to worry; it is a reason to maintain. In San Diego the homes that cost their owners least are the ones where the heater is flushed yearly, the outdoor lines are checked before the hard season, and a small leak is fixed while it is still small.

In depth

The problems San Diego homes face — in depth

Every city has a short list of faults that come up again and again. For San Diego it is the five below, and each one has a cause you can understand, a fix that works, and a habit that prevents the next one.

Ageing supply lines in older coastal homes

Why it happens in San Diego: many pre-1970s houses still run on galvanised pipe that narrows with corrosion and cuts water pressure, and the salt air speeds the outside of the same pipes along.

How we fix it — and how to prevent the next one: the visit starts with a pressure check at more than one tap and a look at the pipe material. A single failed section can be replaced, but where the whole run is narrowed, the durable fix is a planned repipe in copper or PEX, done in stages if needed so the house is never without water for long. Prevention is a pressure check once a year: a slow fall over two visits is the warning to plan the repipe before a joint fails.

Storm drains overwhelmed by heavy rain

Why it happens in San Diego: rain here comes in short, sharp downpours, and when gutters and surface drains are even partly blocked, low spots flood fast.

How we fix it — and how to prevent the next one: we clear the gutters, downspouts and surface drains, then check that water runs away from the house rather than towards it. Where a drain is undersized for the roof it serves, we say so plainly and price the correction. Prevention is a clear-out before the wet season and after the first big storm, when leaves and grit have had their say.

Rust on exposed pipework

Why it happens in San Diego: within a few miles of the shore, bare metal fittings can show rust within a year or two of being fitted, and a rusted fitting is a leak waiting for a date.

How we fix it — and how to prevent the next one: the corroded fitting is replaced with a corrosion-resistant one, and the surrounding pipework is checked for the same weakness. Where an outdoor line is exposed, we fit protection so the next fitting lasts longer than the last. Prevention is a yearly look at every outdoor tap, heater connection and exposed joint — five minutes with a torch, once a year.

Outdoor drains choked with sand

Why it happens in San Diego: coastal grit builds up in yard drains and condensate lines, and the first sign is usually water pooling after rain.

How we fix it — and how to prevent the next one: the drain is cleared and flushed, then flow-tested so the blockage is proved gone rather than just moved along. If the same drain blocks twice, we camera it: a cracked or bellied section holds grit no matter how often it is cleared. Prevention is a flush of yard drains before the wet season and a grate that keeps the worst of the grit out.

Leaks that hide in damp air

Why it happens in San Diego: a slow drip under a coastal home can go unnoticed for months because everything down there is already damp.

How we fix it — and how to prevent the next one: we find the leak with listening gear and a pressure test, then repair the failed section and test the line end to end — no guesswork, no unnecessary digging. Prevention is the simplest habit of all: a look under the house once a year, and a glance at the water meter when nothing is running. A meter that turns when every tap is off is a leak, and it is cheaper to find it now than after the damp has done its work.
