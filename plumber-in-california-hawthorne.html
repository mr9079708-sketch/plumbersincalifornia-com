Know your home

Housing in Hawthorne: what your pipes are made of — and why it matters

A plumber who does not know what your pipes are made of is guessing. In Hawthorne, as across the region, the material depends mostly on when the house was built, and each era has its own failure pattern. This is what we see, era by era.

Before the 1960s: galvanised steel and cast iron

The oldest homes in Hawthorne usually have galvanised steel supply pipes and cast-iron drains. Galvanised steel corrodes from the inside, so the bore narrows year by year: pressure falls, water runs rust-coloured after a holiday, and eventually a joint weeps or a section blocks. Cast-iron drains scale and crack in the same way, and they are the pipes tree roots find first. A repair buys time; a house where the pressure has dropped in every tap is usually ready for a planned repipe in copper or PEX rather than another patch.

1960s to 1980s: copper supply, plastic drains

From the 1960s on, Hawthorne homes usually have copper supply lines and ABS or PVC drains. Copper is a long-lived material, but aggressive water can open pinhole leaks and soldered joints age with the house. The drains are generally sound — the weak point is the sewer lateral, the run to the street, which is often clay or cast iron even in a copper house, and that is where roots get in. A camera inspection answers the question in an hour.

1990s to 2000s: copper and early PEX

In 1990s and 2000s homes in Hawthorne, expect copper and early PEX with PVC drains. The PEX itself is durable and scale-resistant, but the fittings and manifolds from that period should be looked at, and water heaters installed then are now nearing replacement age — worth checking before they fail rather than after.

2010s onwards: PEX and CPVC

Newer homes in Hawthorne are mostly PEX or CPVC, often run from a central manifold so each fixture can be isolated on its own. The pipe itself rarely fails; when something does go wrong it is usually a fitting, a valve or an appliance connection. Tankless water heaters are common in this era too, and they need descaling on a schedule — in hard-water country, skipping it is the fastest way to a cold shower.

How to tell what you have: look at an exposed pipe near the water heater or under a sink. Galvanised steel is dull grey, magnetic and joined with threads; copper is reddish-brown with soldered joints; PEX is flexible plastic, often red for hot and blue for cold; CPVC is rigid cream-coloured plastic.

What we check on a visit in Hawthorne: the pipe material and its condition, water pressure at more than one tap, the water heater's age and anode, and every visible joint for weeping. The report is plain: what is fine, what needs watching, and what should be planned before it fails.

Local problem patterns

Plumbing problems common on the California coast

Local conditions write the plumbing story in Hawthorne. Around here, the same five faults appear year after year — the list below, and the detail on each one further down the page.

  • Leaks that hide in damp air — a slow drip under a coastal home can go unnoticed for months because everything down there is already damp.
  • Low water pressure from corroded lines — internal rust narrows old galvanised pipes until showers run weak and taps slow to a trickle.
  • Gutters and downspouts working overtime — sea storms dump a lot of water fast, and a blocked gutter sends it straight down the wall and into the foundations.
  • Outdoor showers and pool equipment — coastal homes often have outdoor showers and pool plumbing, and their exposed fittings corrode faster than indoor ones.
  • Irrigation and garden lines — watering systems run all year near the coast, and a cracked line or backflow fault can waste water for weeks before anyone notices.

Most of these are cheap to prevent and expensive to ignore — a yearly check of outdoor pipework and drains catches the majority before they fail.
