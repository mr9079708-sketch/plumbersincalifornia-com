What your home’s age means

What Castro Valley’s homes mean for your plumbing

Two houses on the same street in Castro Valley can have completely different plumbing, because the year they were built decides what is in the walls. Knowing what era a home belongs to tells you what to check, what is likely to fail first, and whether a repair or a replacement is the honest answer.

Built before 1960: galvanised steel and cast iron

A pre-1960 home in Castro Valley usually runs on galvanised steel supply pipes and cast-iron drains, and both wear from the inside out. The supply lines rust and narrow, so pressure falls and water runs brown after the house has stood empty; the drains scale and sag, so they block more often and clear more slowly. The work on a home of this age is usually staged: test the lines, replace the worst run first, and plan the rest over a year or two rather than all at once.

1960s to 1980s: copper supply, plastic drains

The 1960s to 1980s brought copper supply lines and plastic drains, and most of that work is still sound in Castro Valley. Copper's weakness is pinhole leaks where the water chemistry is aggressive, and the drains' weakness is age — joints and fittings from this era are now old enough to fail. The checks are targeted: pressure, visible joints, and the busiest bathrooms first.

1990s to today: PEX and CPVC

Homes built from the 1990s onward usually have PEX or CPVC supply lines — flexible, resistant to freezing and scale, and quick to install. They are not trouble-free: fittings matter, ultraviolet light degrades exposed runs, and a bad manifold installation causes pressure problems. The checks here are different — fittings, manifolds and the water heater — rather than the pipes themselves.

In the Bay Area, the ground adds a factor no era escapes: expansive clay soils swell in winter and shrink in summer, working joints open and bellying buried sewer laterals. A camera inspection of the lateral is worth doing on any older home here, whatever the supply pipes are made of.

Common plumbing problems in Castro Valley, in depth

The four problems above are the ones we see most often in Castro Valley. Below is what prevents each of them, plus two more faults that the Bay Area conditions around Castro Valley produce regularly, covered in full.

01

Frequent pipe bursts during winter

How to prevent it: Replace supply lines and washers before they fail: a braided supply line that is ten years old is a burst waiting to happen, and it is a small job to change.

02

Clogged drains in older homes

How to prevent it: Find the main shut-off valve before you need it, and make sure everyone in the house knows where it is. In a leak, the first minute matters more than the first hour.

03

Water heater failures

How to prevent it: If the heater is over ten years old and has never been flushed, book the flush and the anode check together — it is the cheapest way to find out how much life is left.

04

Sewer line backups

How to prevent it: Do not keep clearing the same drain: a fault that returns is telling you the cause is still there, and a camera inspection will find it.

05

Slab leaks under concrete floors

Why it happens: Many Bay Area homes sit on concrete slabs, and a pinhole leak in a buried hot-water line shows up as a warm patch on the floor, a faint sound of running water, or a water bill that climbs for no reason.

How we fix it: We locate the leak with acoustic and electronic detection, then reroute the line overhead or make a spot repair — whichever does less damage to the home.

How to prevent it: Know your normal water bill. A sudden rise with no change in use is the earliest sign of a slab leak, and catching it early is the difference between a reroute and a ruined floor.

06

Leaking washing-machine and dishwasher hoses

Why it happens: Rubber supply hoses perish with age and heat, and when one bursts it can flood a room in minutes — usually while nobody is home.

How we fix it: We replace rubber hoses with braided steel, fit shut-offs, and check the connections. A burst hose is one of the most expensive small failures in a home.

How to prevent it: Check hoses once a year for bulges, cracks or rust at the fittings, and turn the supply off when you go away for more than a few days.
