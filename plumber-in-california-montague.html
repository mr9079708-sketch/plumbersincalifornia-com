Know your home

Housing in Montague: what your pipes are made of — and why it matters

Houses in Montague were not all plumbed the same way. Four eras of pipework are still in daily use across the region, and each one ages differently. Here is how to read your own home.

Before the 1960s: galvanised steel and cast iron

Pre-1960s homes in Montague were plumbed in galvanised steel for supply and cast iron for drains, and both materials age the same way — from the inside. Corrosion narrows galvanised pipe until pressure drops and water discolours; cast iron scales, cracks and invites root intrusion. Patching works for a while, but when the whole house has lost pressure, a planned repipe in copper or PEX is the durable answer.

1960s to 1980s: copper supply, plastic drains

Homes built from the 1960s onwards in Montague usually run copper supply lines with ABS or PVC drains. Copper lasts well, but it is not immortal: aggressive water can cause pinhole leaks, and soldered joints age. The drains from this era are generally sound, though the sewer lateral — the run from the house to the street — is often clay or cast iron even when the house itself is copper, and that is where roots get in. A camera inspection settles the question in an hour.

1990s to 2000s: copper and early PEX

The 1990s and 2000s brought early PEX alongside a lot more copper, with PVC drains throughout. PEX from this period resists scale well, which suits Montague, but early fittings and manifolds deserve inspection — a manifold weeping slowly behind a panel is easy to miss. Heaters from this era are also reaching the end of their working lives, so a heater check is worth doing even while the hot water still runs.

2010s onwards: PEX and CPVC

Newer building in Montague means PEX or CPVC, usually from a manifold so each fixture can be shut off separately. Failures are uncommon and usually sit at a fitting or valve rather than in a run of pipe. Tankless water heaters are frequent in this era and need regular descaling; where the water is hard, that schedule is not optional.

How to tell what you have: look at an exposed pipe near the water heater or under a sink. Galvanised steel is dull grey, magnetic and joined with threads; copper is reddish-brown with soldered joints; PEX is flexible plastic, often red for hot and blue for cold; CPVC is rigid cream-coloured plastic.

What we check on a visit in Montague: the pipe material and its condition, water pressure at more than one tap, the water heater's age and anode, and every visible joint for weeping. The report is plain: what is fine, what needs watching, and what should be planned before it fails.

  • Cabin plumbing left unused — holiday cabins and second homes that sit empty through winter often return to burst pipes and stale, discoloured water in spring.
  • Septic systems under snow — frozen ground and heavy spring melt can overwhelm a septic drain field, and the first sign is slow drains inside.
  • Frozen and burst pipes — water left in uninsulated pipes in garages, crawl spaces and exterior walls freezes, expands and splits the pipe.
  • Snowmelt overloading drains — spring thaw sends a surge through gutters, drains and sump systems that a partly blocked line cannot carry.
  • Well and septic strain — properties on wells and septic systems need pumps and tanks checked before winter, not after they fail in it.

Insulate before the first freeze, clear the gutters in autumn, and have the well pump checked yearly — the mountain routine that prevents most winter emergencies.
