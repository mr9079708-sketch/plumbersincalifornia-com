Know your home

Housing in Lancaster: what your pipes are made of — and why it matters

A plumber who does not know what your pipes are made of is guessing. In Lancaster, as across the region, the material depends mostly on when the house was built, and each era has its own failure pattern. This is what we see, era by era.

Before the 1960s: galvanised steel and cast iron

The oldest homes in Lancaster usually have galvanised steel supply pipes and cast-iron drains. Galvanised steel corrodes from the inside, so the bore narrows year by year: pressure falls, water runs rust-coloured after a holiday, and eventually a joint weeps or a section blocks. Cast-iron drains scale and crack in the same way, and they are the pipes tree roots find first. A repair buys time; a house where the pressure has dropped in every tap is usually ready for a planned repipe in copper or PEX rather than another patch.

1960s to 1980s: copper supply, plastic drains

From the 1960s on, Lancaster homes usually have copper supply lines and ABS or PVC drains. Copper is a long-lived material, but aggressive water can open pinhole leaks and soldered joints age with the house. The drains are generally sound — the weak point is the sewer lateral, the run to the street, which is often clay or cast iron even in a copper house, and that is where roots get in. A camera inspection answers the question in an hour.

1990s to 2000s: copper and early PEX

In 1990s and 2000s homes in Lancaster, expect copper and early PEX with PVC drains. The PEX itself is durable and scale-resistant, but the fittings and manifolds from that period should be looked at, and water heaters installed then are now nearing replacement age — worth checking before they fail rather than after.

2010s onwards: PEX and CPVC

Newer homes in Lancaster are mostly PEX or CPVC, often run from a central manifold so each fixture can be isolated on its own. The pipe itself rarely fails; when something does go wrong it is usually a fitting, a valve or an appliance connection. Tankless water heaters are common in this era too, and they need descaling on a schedule — in hard-water country, skipping it is the fastest way to a cold shower.

How to tell what you have: look at an exposed pipe near the water heater or under a sink. Galvanised steel is dull grey, magnetic and joined with threads; copper is reddish-brown with soldered joints; PEX is flexible plastic, often red for hot and blue for cold; CPVC is rigid cream-coloured plastic.

What we check on a visit in Lancaster: the pipe material and its condition, water pressure at more than one tap, the water heater's age and anode, and every visible joint for weeping. The report is plain: what is fine, what needs watching, and what should be planned before it fails.

Local conditions

Water and climate in Lancaster: what local conditions do to your plumbing

Desert heat is the main enemy of plumbing here. Daytime temperatures expand exposed pipework, ultraviolet light degrades exposed plastic, and outdoor lines left in direct sun crack and leak. Desert nights, especially in the high desert, can turn cold enough to threaten a pipe that was never insulated.

Desert water is very hard. Mineral scale coats water-heater elements and fixtures fast, so heaters run longer, cost more to run and fail earlier without regular flushing. Scale is the reason a desert water heater rarely reaches the age its warranty suggests.

What that means for a homeowner in Lancaster is simple: the local conditions set the maintenance schedule. A heater flush once a year, a look at exposed fittings each spring, and a check of outdoor lines before the season that stresses them most — those three habits prevent most of the expensive calls we get.

In depth

The problems Lancaster homes face — in depth

The region-problem list on this page names the faults Lancaster sees most. Below is each one in depth — why it happens here, how we fix it, and how to stop it coming back.

Leaks at hose bibs and irrigation connections

Why it happens in Lancaster: the most-used outdoor fittings are also the most heat-stressed, and they drip long before anyone checks.

How we fix it — and how to prevent the next one: We replace the worn bib or connection and check the rest of the outdoor fittings, because the most-used ones are also the most heat-stressed. A drip you can hear is already costing water; a drip you cannot hear is costing more.

Pool and spa plumbing

Why it happens in Lancaster: desert homes often have pools, and their pumps, heaters and exposed lines face the same heat and scale as the house plumbing.

How we fix it — and how to prevent the next one: We check the pump, heater and exposed lines for heat and scale damage, then repair the weak point and set a service interval. Pool plumbing faces the same heat and scale as the house — it just gets checked less often.

Evaporative cooler water lines

Why it happens in Lancaster: swamp coolers are common in desert homes, and their supply lines and float valves leak or scale up every season.

How we fix it — and how to prevent the next one: We replace the scaled float valve and supply line, then set the cooler up for the season. Cooler lines need a look every spring before the heat arrives — a leaking float valve can waste water all summer before anyone notices.

Heat expansion in exposed pipes

Why it happens in Lancaster: desert days make above-ground pipework expand and contract daily, loosening joints and splitting fittings.

How we fix it — and how to prevent the next one: We replace the split fitting and re-support the run so it can expand and contract without working joints loose. In desert heat, above-ground pipework needs room to move — rigid runs are the ones that split.

UV damage to plastic lines

Why it happens in Lancaster: sun-baked PVC and hose lines turn brittle and crack, often at the worst moment in a heatwave.

How we fix it — and how to prevent the next one: We replace the brittle line with UV-rated pipe and shield the exposed runs that are still sound. Sun-baked PVC turns brittle and cracks without warning — usually in a heatwave, when you need the water most.

Statewide: know where the main water shut-off is, and consider an earthquake shut-off valve on the gas line — a standard precaution across California. Fixing drips promptly also matters everywhere, since water is precious in this state.

Local water & water heaters

Heat expands exposed pipework, sunlight degrades exposed plastic, and outdoor lines left in the sun crack and leak. Desert water is also very hard, so scale builds up fast inside water heaters. Insulating and shading exposed lines, and flushing the heater every year, are the two habits that matter most in desert homes.

A standard tank water heater typically lasts 8 to 12 years, and in Lancaster the local water is the deciding factor: scale shortens its life, so a heater that is flushed every year usually outlasts one that never is. Rust-coloured water, rumbling noises and falling hot-water pressure are the signs that replacement should be planned rather than left to fail.

Local problem patterns

Plumbing problems common in the California desert

Local conditions write the plumbing story in Lancaster. Around here, the same five faults appear year after year — the list below, and the detail on each one further down the page.

  • Leaks at hose bibs and irrigation connections — the most-used outdoor fittings are also the most heat-stressed, and they drip long before anyone checks.
  • Pool and spa plumbing — desert homes often have pools, and their pumps, heaters and exposed lines face the same heat and scale as the house plumbing.
  • Evaporative cooler water lines — swamp coolers are common in desert homes, and their supply lines and float valves leak or scale up every season.
  • Heat expansion in exposed pipes — desert days make above-ground pipework expand and contract daily, loosening joints and splitting fittings.
  • UV damage to plastic lines — sun-baked PVC and hose lines turn brittle and crack, often at the worst moment in a heatwave.

Insulate or shade what sits outdoors, flush the heater, and keep the drains clear of sand — the desert checklist.
