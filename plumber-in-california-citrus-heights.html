What your home’s age means

What Citrus Heights’s homes mean for your plumbing

The age of a house tells you more about its plumbing than almost anything else. A home built before 1960, one built in the 1970s, and one built last year have different pipes, different failure points and different jobs waiting for them — and in Citrus Heights all three sit on the same streets. This is what the age of a home in Citrus Heights usually means for the plumbing inside it.

Built before 1960: galvanised steel and cast iron

Homes built before about 1960 usually have galvanised steel supply pipes and cast-iron drains. Galvanised steel rusts from the inside, so the bore narrows over decades: pressure falls, water runs brown after a holiday away, and eventually a pinhole leak appears. Cast-iron drains scale and sag in the same way. A home of this age in Citrus Heights is worth a proper look at the pipework, even if nothing has failed yet — the first signs are easy to miss.

1960s to 1980s: copper supply, plastic drains

Homes from the 1960s to the 1980s usually have copper supply lines and ABS or PVC drains — a big step up. Copper lasts well, but it can develop pinhole leaks where the water is aggressive or the pipe was poorly installed, and the drains from this era are now reaching the age where joints and fittings start to fail. The work here is usually targeted: replace the worst run, not the whole system.

1990s to today: PEX and CPVC

PEX and CPVC define homes built from the 1990s onward: flexible pipe that resists freezing and scale, and installs quickly. The weak points moved, they did not vanish — fittings, manifolds and the quality of the installation now decide whether the system is trouble-free. Exposed runs still need protection from sun, and a bad manifold will show up as pressure problems in one part of the house and not another.

In the Valley, hard water is the thread running through all three eras: it scales galvanised lines, clogs copper fittings and settles in PEX manifolds alike. Whatever the pipe material, a water heater that is flushed annually and an anode rod that is replaced on schedule will outlast one that is never serviced.

Preventing clogged drains due to tree roots

Slab leaks under concrete floors

Why it happens: Many California homes sit on concrete slabs, and a pinhole leak in a buried hot-water line shows up as a warm patch on the floor, a faint sound of running water, or a water bill that climbs for no reason.

How we fix it: We locate the leak with acoustic and electronic detection, then reroute the line overhead or make a spot repair — whichever does less damage to the home.

How to prevent it: Know your normal water bill. A sudden rise with no change in use is the earliest sign of a slab leak, and catching it early is the difference between a reroute and a ruined floor.

06
