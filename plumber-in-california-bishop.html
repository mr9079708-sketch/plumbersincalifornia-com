What your home’s age means

What Bishop’s homes mean for your plumbing

The age of a house tells you more about its plumbing than almost anything else. A home built before 1960, one built in the 1970s, and one built last year have different pipes, different failure points and different jobs waiting for them — and in Bishop all three sit on the same streets. This is what the age of a home in Bishop usually means for the plumbing inside it.

Built before 1960: galvanised steel and cast iron

Homes built before about 1960 usually have galvanised steel supply pipes and cast-iron drains. Galvanised steel rusts from the inside, so the bore narrows over decades: pressure falls, water runs brown after a holiday away, and eventually a pinhole leak appears. Cast-iron drains scale and sag in the same way. A home of this age in Bishop is worth a proper look at the pipework, even if nothing has failed yet — the first signs are easy to miss.

1960s to 1980s: copper supply, plastic drains

Homes from the 1960s to the 1980s usually have copper supply lines and ABS or PVC drains — a big step up. Copper lasts well, but it can develop pinhole leaks where the water is aggressive or the pipe was poorly installed, and the drains from this era are now reaching the age where joints and fittings start to fail. The work here is usually targeted: replace the worst run, not the whole system.

1990s to today: PEX and CPVC

From the 1990s onward, PEX and CPVC took over: flexible, freeze-resistant, quick to install. The failure points moved to the fittings and the manifold, and to exposed runs left in the sun. A home of this era in Bishop rarely needs repiping — it needs its fittings, its manifold and its water heater checked, which is a smaller job and a shorter visit.

In the mountains, exposure matters more than era: a pipe in an unheated garage or crawl space will freeze whatever it is made of. Insulating exposed runs, shutting off exterior taps before the first cold snap, and leaving a little heat on in unused rooms matter more here than any pipe material.

Water and weather in Bishop

Water and weather in Bishop: what it does to plumbing

Plumbing fails for local reasons. In Bishop the local reasons are freezing winters, snow and freeze-thaw cycles, plus cold, snowy winters and warm, dry summers — and understanding them is the difference between a repair that lasts and one that comes back next season.

Cold is the mountain's plumbing problem, and it is a patient one. A pipe does not have to freeze solid to fail: repeated freeze-thaw cycles work joints loose over a winter, and the split often appears at the thaw, hours after the cold has passed. The vulnerable runs are the forgotten ones — outdoor taps, pipes in unheated garages and crawl spaces, lines to garden buildings. Insulating them and shutting off exterior taps before the first cold snap prevents almost all of it.

Spring in the mountains is a drainage test. Snowmelt saturates the ground, gutters that spent the winter full of leaves overflow, and sump systems get their hardest workout of the year. Clearing gutters in the autumn, keeping downspouts running away from the house, and testing any sump pump before the melt starts will keep the season uneventful. The first heavy melt is the wrong time to discover a pump has seized.

Water in the mountains is generally soft to moderately hard, depending on the source, so scale is a smaller worry here than freeze damage — but winter still strains water heaters, because cold inlet water means the heater works harder and longer to reach temperature. A scaled tank or a failing thermostat that coped fine in summer will show up in January. Flushing the tank in the autumn, before the cold inlet water arrives, is the mountain version of the annual service.

Local problem patterns

Plumbing problems common in the Sierra Nevada and mountain towns

Mountain plumbing is a winter story. Freezing temperatures and snowmelt cause most of the trouble in Bishop.

  • Frozen outdoor taps — a hose left connected over winter traps water in the bib, which freezes, splits and leaks when it thaws.
  • Cabin plumbing left unused — holiday cabins and second homes that sit empty through winter often return to burst pipes and stale, discoloured water in spring.
  • Septic systems under snow — frozen ground and heavy spring melt can overwhelm a septic drain field, and the first sign is slow drains inside.
  • Frozen and burst pipes — water left in uninsulated pipes in garages, crawl spaces and exterior walls freezes, expands and splits the pipe.
  • Snowmelt overloading drains — spring thaw sends a surge through gutters, drains and sump systems that a partly blocked line cannot carry.

Disconnect hoses before winter, insulate exposed lines, and test the sump pump in autumn — three small jobs that prevent the big mountain call-outs.
