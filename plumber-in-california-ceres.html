What your home’s age means

What Ceres’s homes mean for your plumbing

Two houses on the same street in Ceres can have completely different plumbing, because the year they were built decides what is in the walls. Knowing what era a home belongs to tells you what to check, what is likely to fail first, and whether a repair or a replacement is the honest answer.

Built before 1960: galvanised steel and cast iron

Homes built before about 1960 usually have galvanised steel supply pipes and cast-iron drains. Galvanised steel rusts from the inside, so the bore narrows over decades: pressure falls, water runs brown after a holiday away, and eventually a pinhole leak appears. Cast-iron drains scale and sag in the same way. A home of this age in Ceres is worth a proper look at the pipework, even if nothing has failed yet — the first signs are easy to miss.

1960s to 1980s: copper supply, plastic drains

Copper and plastic define the 1960s to 1980s: copper supply lines, ABS or PVC drains. It was a good era for plumbing, and most of it is still sound — but copper can develop pinhole leaks where the water chemistry is aggressive, and the drains are now old enough for joints and fittings to fail. The checks here are targeted: pressure, visible joints, and the drains that serve the busiest bathrooms.

1990s to today: PEX and CPVC

PEX and CPVC define homes built from the 1990s onward: flexible pipe that resists freezing and scale, and installs quickly. The weak points moved, they did not vanish — fittings, manifolds and the quality of the installation now decide whether the system is trouble-free. Exposed runs still need protection from sun, and a bad manifold will show up as pressure problems in one part of the house and not another.

In the Valley, hard water is the thread running through all three eras: it scales galvanised lines, clogs copper fittings and settles in PEX manifolds alike. Whatever the pipe material, a water heater that is flushed annually and an anode rod that is replaced on schedule will outlast one that is never serviced.

Common plumbing problems in Ceres, in depth

01

02

How to prevent it: Run water in every sink, shower and bath at least once a month, including the ones nobody uses. A trap that stays full is a trap that keeps smells out.

06

Slab leaks under concrete floors

Why it happens: Many California homes sit on concrete slabs, and a pinhole leak in a buried hot-water line shows up as a warm patch on the floor, a faint sound of running water, or a water bill that climbs for no reason.

How we fix it: We locate the leak with acoustic and electronic detection, then reroute the line overhead or make a spot repair — whichever does less damage to the home.

How to prevent it: Know your normal water bill. A sudden rise with no change in use is the earliest sign of a slab leak, and catching it early is the difference between a reroute and a ruined floor.

Water and weather in Ceres

Water and weather in Ceres: what it does to plumbing

Plumbing fails for local reasons. In Ceres the local reasons are hard water and long, hot summers, plus very hot, dry summers and cool, foggy winters — and understanding them is the difference between a repair that lasts and one that comes back next season.

Hard water is the Valley's slow tax on plumbing. Every litre leaves a little mineral behind, and over years that mineral narrows pipes, clogs aerators and coats the inside of the water heater until it heats half as well for twice the running cost. The visible sign is white scale on taps and showerheads; the expensive sign is a heater that rumbles, runs out of hot water early, or fails years before its time. Flushing the tank once a year is the single most valuable maintenance job in Ceres.

Heat is the Valley's acute problem. Long summers expand exposed supply lines, bake hoses and irrigation connections, and crack the plastic fittings left in direct sun. The failures cluster in August, and they are found as damp patches by the house wall or bills that climb for no reason. Shading or lagging exposed runs before the heat peaks takes an hour.

The Valley's other signature is dust. Dry-season dust and field debris find their way into outdoor drains and gutters, and sit there until the first autumn rain washes them into a solid blockage. A clear-out before the first rain is good practice, and it is the cheapest drainage job of the year. The same dust gets into irrigation filters and outdoor tap aerators, so a weak outdoor tap in September is usually a clogged filter, not a supply problem.

Plumbing problems common in the Central Valley
