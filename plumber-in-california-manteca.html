Know your home

Housing in Manteca: what your pipes are made of — and why it matters

Ask a plumber in Manteca what matters most on a first visit and the answer is usually the same: the age of the house and the material of its pipes. The two are linked, and knowing the link tells you what is likely to fail next.

Before the 1960s: galvanised steel and cast iron

The oldest homes in Manteca usually still have galvanised steel supply pipes and cast-iron drains. Inside, galvanised steel corrodes and the bore narrows year by year: pressure falls, the water runs rust-coloured after a holiday, and in time a joint weeps or a section blocks. Cast-iron drains scale and crack the same way, and tree roots find them first. A repair buys time, but a house where pressure has dropped at every tap is usually ready for a planned repipe in copper or PEX.

1960s to 1980s: copper supply, plastic drains

Homes built in the 1960s, 70s and 80s in Manteca are typically copper for supply and plastic for drains. Copper holds up well, though pinhole leaks from aggressive water and ageing soldered joints are not unusual. Watch the sewer lateral: even when the house is copper, the lateral to the street is often clay or cast iron, and roots exploit it. A camera survey settles it quickly.

1990s to 2000s: copper and early PEX

The 1990s and 2000s brought the first PEX and a lot more copper, with PVC drains throughout. PEX from this period is flexible and resistant to scale, which suits Manteca well, but early fittings and manifolds deserve a look: a manifold with a slow weep is easy to miss behind a panel. Water heaters from this era are now reaching the end of their working life, so a heater check is worth doing even if the hot water still runs.

2010s onwards: PEX and CPVC

Homes built since the 2010s in Manteca are mostly PEX or CPVC, often run from a central manifold that lets each fixture be isolated on its own. The pipe rarely fails; trouble usually starts at a fitting, a valve or an appliance connection. Tankless heaters are common in these homes too, and they need descaling on schedule — in hard-water country, skipping it is the fastest route to a cold shower.

How to tell what you have: look at an exposed pipe near the water heater or under a sink. Galvanised steel is dull grey, magnetic and joined with threads; copper is reddish-brown with soldered joints; PEX is flexible plastic, often red for hot and blue for cold; CPVC is rigid cream-coloured plastic.

What we check on a visit in Manteca: the pipe material and its condition, water pressure at more than one tap, the water heater's age and anode, and every visible joint for weeping. The report is plain: what is fine, what needs watching, and what should be planned before it fails.

The faults below are the ones Manteca residents call about most. They follow from the local water, climate and housing, so they recur until the cause is dealt with — which is why each one gets a full treatment below.

  • Heat-stressed outdoor lines — long hot summers crack hose bibs, irrigation connections and any exposed pipe left in direct sun.
  • Ageing galvanised supply lines — older Valley homes often still run on steel pipe that corrodes internally and throttles pressure.
  • High summer demand — garden watering and long showers push older systems hard, and weak points tend to fail in July and August.
  • Water heater sediment — mineral sediment settles at the bottom of the tank, making the heater work harder, sound louder and die younger.
  • Furred-up taps and showerheads — white scale on fixtures is the visible half of the problem; the same scale is narrowing the pipes behind the wall.

Most Valley plumbing trouble is mineral trouble. A yearly heater flush and a look at the fixtures buys years of quiet service.
