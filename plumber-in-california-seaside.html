Seasonal Considerations for Plumbing in Seaside

Each season stresses different parts of a Seaside plumbing system. This is the yearly pattern, and the preparation that keeps it uneventful.

Spring

Spring on the coast is the season to check what winter storms did. Salt corrosion on outdoor fittings, sand in outdoor drains and damp patches under the house all show up once the rain eases.

Tip: Rinse exposed outdoor fittings and check for salt corrosion in spring, and clear sand from outdoor drains.

Summer

Summer brings fog, visitors and heavy use of outdoor showers and hoses. Outdoor plumbing works hardest in these months, and salt air never takes a season off, so a fitting that looked fine in May can be crusted by August.

Tip: Check outdoor showers, hose bibs and exposed fittings mid-summer, and replace any washer that has gone stiff.

Fall

Autumn is preparation season on the coast. The first winter storm arrives off the ocean with driving rain, and it finds every gutter, downpipe and outdoor drain that was left uncleared after summer.

Tip: Clear gutters, downpipes and outdoor drains in October, before the first storm of the season.

Winter

Winter storms are the coast's real test for plumbing. Driving rain fills drains in hours, damp peaks, and any slow leak inside a wall has months of moist air to turn into mould or rot.

Tip: Check the sump pump and outdoor drains before the first storm, and treat any musty smell as a leak until proven otherwise.

Housing and pipework in Seaside

Local Housing and Plumbing Context in Seaside

How old a house is usually tells you what its pipes are made of, and what is likely to go wrong with them. Seaside homes in Monterey County span several build eras, so it helps to know which era your home belongs to.

Before the 1960s: galvanised steel. The oldest homes in the area may still carry galvanised steel supply lines. Galvanised pipe rusts from the inside, so the bore narrows year by year, pressure falls gradually, and the water can run brown after the line has sat unused overnight. There is no repair that reverses this: once galvanised pipe is rusting through, the permanent fix is replacement, and a planned re-pipe costs far less than an emergency one after a burst.

1960s to 1980s: copper. Copper dominates the middle era, and it is durable, generally serving well for decades. Its weaknesses are specific: pinhole leaks where the water is aggressive or a run sits unused, and slab leaks where a copper run under a concrete floor has been weeping unnoticed. A damp patch, a warm spot on the floor, or a water bill that climbs for no reason are the signs to act on, and catching them early is the difference between a localised repair and a major job.

1990s onward: PEX and CPVC. Newer homes generally use plastic pipework, which does not corrode, does not scale up the way metal does, and flexes rather than bursting in a freeze. Its weak points are different: fittings and manifolds that deserve a periodic check, and any run left exposed to sun or weather, which becomes brittle over time. Plastic is not a fit-and-forget material, it is simply a material whose failures look different.

What this means in Seaside. Coastal housing mixes old cottages with newer builds, and the pipe era usually follows the build date. The oldest homes may still have galvanised steel, which rusts from within and narrows until pressure drops and the water runs brown, and coastal damp rusts the outside of those pipes as well. Homes from the middle decades are generally copper, which lasts well but can develop pinhole leaks, especially where salt air reaches exposed runs. Newer homes use plastic pipework, which never rusts, though fittings still deserve a look, and any plastic left out in sun and salt spray goes brittle faster than it would inland.

What to ask the technician. While the technician is on site, three questions are worth asking: what material are the supply lines, how old is the water heater, and do the shut-off valves actually turn? Those three answers say more about the next five years of your plumbing than any inspection report, and they cost nothing to find out while the work is already under way.

Sand and debris blocking outdoor drains

Cause: Sand washes into outdoor drains, shower traps and laundry lines, settles in bends, and narrows the line a little more with every season, until a drain that was merely slow blocks completely.

What we do: We clear the line, flush it through, and check the fall of the drain so water runs away freely instead of pooling around the house.

How to prevent it: Fit strainers on outdoor drains and shower wastes, and flush outdoor lines clear before the winter storms arrive.

02

Damp crawl spaces and hidden leaks

Cause: Coastal damp keeps crawl spaces moist for much of the year, so a slow leak inside a wall or under a floor has ideal conditions to turn into rot or mould long before anyone sees water.

What we do: We trace the leak with moisture detection, repair the section, and advise on ventilation for the space so the repair lasts.

How to prevent it: Look under the house once a year, and treat any musty smell or damp patch as a leak until proven otherwise.

03

Water heaters corroding in damp, salty air

Cause: A water heater in a damp coastal garage works in the worst conditions in the house: moist, salty air corrodes the casing and connections, and sediment settles in the tank.

What we do: We flush the tank, check the anode rod, burner and venting, and test recovery time so you know whether a service has fixed it or replacement is the honest answer.

How to prevent it: Flush the heater once a year and keep the space around it as dry and ventilated as possible. A heater that rumbles or recovers slowly is asking for a service.

04

Tree roots getting into older sewer lines

Cause: Coastal gardens are heavily planted, and roots follow moisture straight to the sewer lateral. Older clay and concrete joints let them in, and once inside they trap waste until the line backs up.

What we do: We camera-inspect the line to find the intrusion, clear it mechanically, and set out the repair choices, including trenchless options that avoid digging up a garden.

How to prevent it: Camera-check the lateral every couple of years if large trees or shrubs stand near it, and avoid planting thirsty species over the sewer run.
