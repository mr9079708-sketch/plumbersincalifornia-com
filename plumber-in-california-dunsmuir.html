What your home’s age means

What Dunsmuir’s homes mean for your plumbing

The age of a house tells you more about its plumbing than almost anything else. A home built before 1960, one built in the 1970s, and one built last year have different pipes, different failure points and different jobs waiting for them — and in Dunsmuir all three sit on the same streets. This is what the age of a home in Dunsmuir usually means for the plumbing inside it.

Built before 1960: galvanised steel and cast iron

A pre-1960 home in Dunsmuir usually runs on galvanised steel supply pipes and cast-iron drains, and both wear from the inside out. The supply lines rust and narrow, so pressure falls and water runs brown after the house has stood empty; the drains scale and sag, so they block more often and clear more slowly. The work on a home of this age is usually staged: test the lines, replace the worst run first, and plan the rest over a year or two rather than all at once.

1960s to 1980s: copper supply, plastic drains

The 1960s to 1980s brought copper supply lines and plastic drains, and most of that work is still sound in Dunsmuir. Copper's weakness is pinhole leaks where the water chemistry is aggressive, and the drains' weakness is age — joints and fittings from this era are now old enough to fail. The checks are targeted: pressure, visible joints, and the busiest bathrooms first.

1990s to today: PEX and CPVC

Homes built from the 1990s onward usually have PEX or CPVC supply lines — flexible, resistant to freezing and scale, and quick to install. They are not trouble-free: fittings matter, ultraviolet light degrades exposed runs, and a bad manifold installation causes pressure problems. The checks here are different — fittings, manifolds and the water heater — rather than the pipes themselves.

In the mountains, exposure matters more than era: a pipe in an unheated garage or crawl space will freeze whatever it is made of. Insulating exposed runs, shutting off exterior taps before the first cold snap, and leaving a little heat on in unused rooms matter more here than any pipe material.

Water heater strain in winter

Why it happens: Cold inlet water in winter means the heater works harder and longer to reach temperature, which exposes a scaled tank or a failing thermostat that coped fine in summer.

How we fix it: We flush the tank, test the thermostat and the anode rod, and check the venting — a heater that cannot keep up in January usually needs a flush, not a replacement.

How to prevent it: Flush the tank in the autumn, before the cold inlet water arrives. If the hot water runs short only in winter, that is a capacity problem, not a broken heater.

Water and weather in Dunsmuir

Water and weather in Dunsmuir: what it does to plumbing

Plumbing fails for local reasons. In Dunsmuir the local reasons are freezing winters, snow and freeze-thaw cycles, plus cold, snowy winters and warm, dry summers — and understanding them is the difference between a repair that lasts and one that comes back next season.

Cold is the mountain's plumbing problem, and it is a patient one. A pipe does not have to freeze solid to fail: repeated freeze-thaw cycles work joints loose over a winter, and the split often appears at the thaw, hours after the cold has passed. The vulnerable runs are the forgotten ones — outdoor taps, pipes in unheated garages and crawl spaces, lines to garden buildings. Insulating them and shutting off exterior taps before the first cold snap prevents almost all of it.

Freeze-thaw is the second mountain factor. Water expands when it freezes and contracts as it thaws, and that cycle works joints loose over a winter — especially on older copper and galvanised runs, and on plastic fittings left in the cold. A pipe that survived last winter can fail this one, so a pre-winter check of exposed pipework, hose bibs and the water-heater vent is worth doing every autumn. If a tap gives nothing on a cold morning, do not force it: warm the pipe gently and call us if it stays shut.

Snowmelt brings its own work. Spring runoff saturates the ground and can overwhelm drains and gutters that spent the winter full of leaves and debris, and the first heavy melt is when sump systems and surface drainage get tested. Clearing gutters in the autumn, keeping downspouts running away from the house, and making sure any sump pump is tested before the melt starts will keep the season uneventful. Water in the mountains is generally soft to moderately hard, so scale is a smaller worry here than freeze damage.

Plumbing problems common in the Sierra Nevada and mountain towns

In mountain towns like Dunsmuir, plumbing problems follow the seasons: frozen pipes in winter, meltwater in spring, and debris all autumn.
