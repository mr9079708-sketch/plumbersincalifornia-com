Know your home

Housing in Monterey: what your pipes are made of — and why it matters

A plumber who does not know what your pipes are made of is guessing. In Monterey, as across the region, the material depends mostly on when the house was built, and each era has its own failure pattern. This is what we see, era by era.

Before the 1960s: galvanised steel and cast iron

The oldest homes in Monterey usually have galvanised steel supply pipes and cast-iron drains. Galvanised steel corrodes from the inside, so the bore narrows year by year: pressure falls, water runs rust-coloured after a holiday, and eventually a joint weeps or a section blocks. Cast-iron drains scale and crack in the same way, and they are the pipes tree roots find first. A repair buys time; a house where the pressure has dropped in every tap is usually ready for a planned repipe in copper or PEX rather than another patch.

1960s to 1980s: copper supply, plastic drains

From the 1960s on, Monterey homes usually have copper supply lines and ABS or PVC drains. Copper is a long-lived material, but aggressive water can open pinhole leaks and soldered joints age with the house. The drains are generally sound — the weak point is the sewer lateral, the run to the street, which is often clay or cast iron even in a copper house, and that is where roots get in. A camera inspection answers the question in an hour.

1990s to 2000s: copper and early PEX

In 1990s and 2000s homes in Monterey, expect copper and early PEX with PVC drains. The PEX itself is durable and scale-resistant, but the fittings and manifolds from that period should be looked at, and water heaters installed then are now nearing replacement age — worth checking before they fail rather than after.

2010s onwards: PEX and CPVC

Newer homes in Monterey are mostly PEX or CPVC, often run from a central manifold so each fixture can be isolated on its own. The pipe itself rarely fails; when something does go wrong it is usually a fitting, a valve or an appliance connection. Tankless water heaters are common in this era too, and they need descaling on a schedule — in hard-water country, skipping it is the fastest way to a cold shower.

How to tell what you have: look at an exposed pipe near the water heater or under a sink. Galvanised steel is dull grey, magnetic and joined with threads; copper is reddish-brown with soldered joints; PEX is flexible plastic, often red for hot and blue for cold; CPVC is rigid cream-coloured plastic.

What we check on a visit in Monterey: the pipe material and its condition, water pressure at more than one tap, the water heater's age and anode, and every visible joint for weeping. The report is plain: what is fine, what needs watching, and what should be planned before it fails.

Local conditions

Water and climate in Monterey: what local conditions do to your plumbing

Salt-laden sea air is the defining force on plumbing here. It corrodes exposed brass and copper fittings, outdoor taps and water-heater connections faster than inland air does, and the damp fog that rolls in off the water keeps crawl spaces and under-sink cupboards moist, so a slow leak can run unnoticed for months.

Water supplies along the coast are generally moderately hard. That is enough to leave scale in kettles and showerheads over a few years, and to shorten a water heater's life if it is never flushed, though it is less aggressive than the groundwater of the Central Valley.

What that means for a homeowner in Monterey is simple: the local conditions set the maintenance schedule. A heater flush once a year, a look at exposed fittings each spring, and a check of outdoor lines before the season that stresses them most — those three habits prevent most of the expensive calls we get.

Local problem patterns

Plumbing problems common on the California coast

Local conditions write the plumbing story in Monterey. Around here, the same five faults appear year after year — the list below, and the detail on each one further down the page.

  • Outdoor drains choked with sand — coastal grit builds up in yard drains and condensate lines, and the first sign is usually water pooling after rain.
  • Leaks that hide in damp air — a slow drip under a coastal home can go unnoticed for months because everything down there is already damp.
  • Low water pressure from corroded lines — internal rust narrows old galvanised pipes until showers run weak and taps slow to a trickle.
  • Gutters and downspouts working overtime — sea storms dump a lot of water fast, and a blocked gutter sends it straight down the wall and into the foundations.
  • Outdoor showers and pool equipment — coastal homes often have outdoor showers and pool plumbing, and their exposed fittings corrode faster than indoor ones.

A quick annual look at outdoor taps, drains and gutters is the simplest defence — and it is the visit most coastal homeowners skip.
