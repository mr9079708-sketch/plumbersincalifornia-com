What your home’s age means

What Folsom’s homes mean for your plumbing

Two houses on the same street in Folsom can have completely different plumbing, because the year they were built decides what is in the walls. Knowing what era a home belongs to tells you what to check, what is likely to fail first, and whether a repair or a replacement is the honest answer.

Built before 1960: galvanised steel and cast iron

A pre-1960 home in Folsom usually runs on galvanised steel supply pipes and cast-iron drains, and both wear from the inside out. The supply lines rust and narrow, so pressure falls and water runs brown after the house has stood empty; the drains scale and sag, so they block more often and clear more slowly. The work on a home of this age is usually staged: test the lines, replace the worst run first, and plan the rest over a year or two rather than all at once.

1960s to 1980s: copper supply, plastic drains

Homes from the 1960s to the 1980s usually have copper supply lines and ABS or PVC drains — a big step up. Copper lasts well, but it can develop pinhole leaks where the water is aggressive or the pipe was poorly installed, and the drains from this era are now reaching the age where joints and fittings start to fail. The work here is usually targeted: replace the worst run, not the whole system.

1990s to today: PEX and CPVC

Homes built from the 1990s onward usually have PEX or CPVC supply lines — flexible, resistant to freezing and scale, and quick to install. They are not trouble-free: fittings matter, ultraviolet light degrades exposed runs, and a bad manifold installation causes pressure problems. The checks here are different — fittings, manifolds and the water heater — rather than the pipes themselves.

In the Valley, hard water is the thread running through all three eras: it scales galvanised lines, clogs copper fittings and settles in PEX manifolds alike. Whatever the pipe material, a water heater that is flushed annually and an anode rod that is replaced on schedule will outlast one that is never serviced.

Common plumbing problems in Folsom, in depth

The four problems above are the ones we see most often in Folsom. Below is how to stop each of them coming back, plus two more faults that the Central Valley conditions around Folsom produce regularly, covered in full.

01

Frequent pipe bursts during cold weather

How to prevent it: Insulate exposed pipes before the first cold snap, shut off exterior taps from inside, and leave a little heat on in unused rooms — the three jobs that prevent almost all freeze damage.

02

Clogged drains due to tree roots

How to prevent it: Find the main shut-off valve before you need it, and make sure everyone in the house knows where it is. In a leak, the first minute matters more than the first hour.

03

Water heater failures

How to prevent it: A maintenance visit once a year covers the heater: the tank, the anode rod, the thermostat and the venting, checked before the season that stresses it most.

04

Leaky faucets and fixtures

How to prevent it: Check the places leaks start — under sinks, behind toilets, around the water heater — once a year, and fix a weeping joint before it becomes a stain on the ceiling below.

05

Slab leaks under concrete floors

Why it happens: Many California homes sit on concrete slabs, and a pinhole leak in a buried hot-water line shows up as a warm patch on the floor, a faint sound of running water, or a water bill that climbs for no reason.

How we fix it: We locate the leak with acoustic and electronic detection, then reroute the line overhead or make a spot repair — whichever does less damage to the home.

How to prevent it: Know your normal water bill. A sudden rise with no change in use is the earliest sign of a slab leak, and catching it early is the difference between a reroute and a ruined floor.

06

Sewer odours from dried traps

Why it happens: Traps dry out in unused bathrooms and guest rooms, and heat speeds it up — once the water seal is gone, sewer smells come straight up the drain.

How we fix it: We run water through every trap, check the venting, and find any trap that is not holding its seal. A trap that keeps drying out usually has a venting fault behind it.

How to prevent it: Run water in every sink, shower and bath at least once a month, including the ones nobody uses. A trap that stays full is a trap that keeps smells out.

How Seasons Affect Plumbing in Folsom

Each season stresses different parts of a Folsom plumbing system. This is the yearly pattern, and the preparation that keeps it uneventful.

Spring

Spring is the season to undo winter’s wear: clear the gutters, check the drains, and look over any pipework that spent the winter cold and damp.

Tip: Schedule a plumbing inspection to ensure your drainage systems are clear and functioning properly.

Summer

Summer is the season for outdoor plumbing: hoses, irrigation and the lines that sit in the sun all get used hard, and heat expands every joint in the system.

Tip: Check outdoor taps and hose connections in early summer, and watch the water bill — a rise with no change in use usually means a leak somewhere.

Fall

Autumn is preparation season: gutters cleared before the rain, exposed pipes lagged before the cold, and the water heater flushed before it has to work hardest.

Tip: Book a maintenance check in the autumn — it is the cheapest visit of the year, and it prevents the expensive ones.

Winter

Winter is when the cold tests the plumbing: a cold snap can freeze an exposed pipe, a scaled heater gives up under the extra load, and the first storm tests every drain.

Tip: Insulate exposed pipes before the cold arrives, and keep the house heated — even rooms that are not used much.

Water and weather in Folsom

Water and weather in Folsom: what it does to plumbing

Plumbing fails for local reasons. In Folsom the local reasons are hard water and long, hot summers, plus very hot, dry summers and cool, foggy winters — and understanding them is the difference between a repair that lasts and one that comes back next season.

Water is the defining plumbing factor in Folsom. Supply across the Central Valley is hard, and mineral scale is the result: it narrows pipes, clogs aerators and showerheads, and settles inside water heaters, where it insulates the burner or element from the water. A heater full of scale works harder, costs more to run and dies years early. Flushing the tank once a year and replacing the anode rod on schedule is the cheapest maintenance in plumbing, and in Sacramento County it is the maintenance that matters most.

Heat is the second factor. Long, hot summers expand exposed supply lines and bake hoses, irrigation connections and the plastic fittings on outdoor equipment. Lines that sit in direct sun expand and contract every day, which works joints loose over a season; shading or lagging exposed runs, and checking them before the heat peaks, prevents the split joints and weeping connections we see every August. Dust and field debris also find their way into outdoor drains and gutters during the dry season, so a clear-out before the first autumn rain is good practice.

Dust is the Valley's quiet plumbing problem. Dry-season dust and field debris collect in outdoor drains and gutters, then wash into a solid blockage with the first autumn rain. A clear-out before the first rain is the cheapest drainage job of the year — and the same dust clogs irrigation filters and outdoor tap aerators, so a weak outdoor tap in September is usually a filter, not a supply fault.

Local problem patterns

Plumbing problems common in the Central Valley

The Central Valley is tough on plumbing in two ways: the water is hard and the summers are long. Here is what that means in Folsom.

  • Dust and debris in outdoor drains — dry-season dust and field debris collect in gullies, then the first autumn rain turns them into a blockage.
  • Running toilets wasting water — a worn flapper in a Valley home can waste hundreds of litres a day, and the water bill is usually the first clue.
  • Outdoor taps left running for gardens — Valley gardens need daily watering in summer, and a tap or hose left on can flood a yard or run up the bill.
  • Appliance hookups under strain — dishwashers and washing machines run hard in large Valley households, and their supply hoses are a common leak point.
  • Scale in water heaters — hard water lays down mineral scale inside tanks and on heating elements, cutting efficiency and shortening the heater's life.

Flushing the water heater once a year and checking outdoor lines before summer are the two cheapest habits in Valley plumbing.
