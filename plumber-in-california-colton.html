What your home’s age means

What Colton’s homes mean for your plumbing

Two houses on the same street in Colton can have completely different plumbing, because the year they were built decides what is in the walls. Knowing what era a home belongs to tells you what to check, what is likely to fail first, and whether a repair or a replacement is the honest answer.

Built before 1960: galvanised steel and cast iron

Homes built before about 1960 usually have galvanised steel supply pipes and cast-iron drains. Galvanised steel rusts from the inside, so the bore narrows over decades: pressure falls, water runs brown after a holiday away, and eventually a pinhole leak appears. Cast-iron drains scale and sag in the same way. A home of this age in Colton is worth a proper look at the pipework, even if nothing has failed yet — the first signs are easy to miss.

1960s to 1980s: copper supply, plastic drains

Homes from the 1960s to the 1980s usually have copper supply lines and ABS or PVC drains — a big step up. Copper lasts well, but it can develop pinhole leaks where the water is aggressive or the pipe was poorly installed, and the drains from this era are now reaching the age where joints and fittings start to fail. The work here is usually targeted: replace the worst run, not the whole system.

1990s to today: PEX and CPVC

Homes built from the 1990s onward usually have PEX or CPVC supply lines — flexible, resistant to freezing and scale, and quick to install. They are not trouble-free: fittings matter, ultraviolet light degrades exposed runs, and a bad manifold installation causes pressure problems. The checks here are different — fittings, manifolds and the water heater — rather than the pipes themselves.

Across inland California, the habits matter more than the era: flush the heater annually, replace the anode rod on schedule, descale the aerators when flow drops, and check outdoor lines before the heat peaks. Whatever the pipes are made of, those four jobs are what keep a system healthy.

Spring is when winter's damage shows up: gutters full of debris, drains that slowed over the cold months, and the first heavy rain testing all of it at once.

Tip: Clear the gutters and check the drains before the spring rain arrives — a slow drain in March is a backup in April.

Summer

Summer heat can cause pipes to expand, leading to leaks and pressure issues.

Tip: Keep an eye on your water bill for unexpected increases, which may indicate leaks that need addressing.

Fall

Fall is a time for preparing plumbing for winter, including checking for leaks and insulating pipes.

Tip: Consider scheduling a maintenance check to ensure your plumbing is ready for cooler temperatures.

Winter

Winter is when the cold tests the plumbing: a cold snap can freeze an exposed pipe, a scaled heater gives up under the extra load, and the first storm tests every drain.

Tip: Insulate exposed pipes before the cold arrives, and keep the house heated — even rooms that are not used much.

Water and weather in Colton

Water and weather in Colton: what it does to plumbing

Plumbing fails for local reasons. In Colton the local reasons are hot summers, hard water and older housing stock, plus hot, dry summers and cool, wet winters — and understanding them is the difference between a repair that lasts and one that comes back next season.

Hard water drives most of the maintenance here. Scale builds inside heaters, narrows older pipes and clogs aerators, and a heater that is flushed once a year will reach its expected life while one that is never flushed dies early. The visible sign is white scale on taps; the expensive sign is a heater that gives up years before its time.

Heat works on the outside of the system. Exposed supply lines expand and contract through the summer, which loosens joints and cracks hoses and irrigation connections left in the sun; shading or lagging the exposed runs and checking them before the heat peaks prevents the split joints we see every August. Winter brings the opposite problem in short bursts: a cold snap can freeze an exposed tap or a pipe in an unheated garage, so insulating those runs before winter is cheap insurance.

The ground moves a little here too. A mix of clay and fill that shifts with moisture shift with moisture, working joints open and bellying buried laterals, and roots find the gaps. A camera inspection tells you in one visit whether the line is sound or needs work — worth doing before buying an older home in Colton or after any backup that clears and then comes back.

Local problem patterns

Plumbing problems common across California

Whatever the town, California plumbing fails in familiar ways. In Colton, these five are the ones to watch for.

  • Low pressure from old supply lines — corroded or scaled pipes throttle flow, usually worst at the taps furthest from the main.
  • Sewer blockages from roots and grease — the two most common causes of a main-line backup, and both are preventable with regular attention.
  • Leaks that run up the water bill — a silent toilet leak or a dripping outdoor tap can waste hundreds of litres a day.
  • Outdoor lines damaged by sun and weather — exposed pipework, hose bibs and irrigation connections wear out faster than the pipes inside the walls.
  • Washing machine hoses — rubber supply hoses age and burst without warning, and they are one of the most common causes of indoor flooding.

A yearly check of the heater, the visible pipework and the shut-off valves catches most of these before they become emergencies.
