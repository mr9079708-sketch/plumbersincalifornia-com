Know your home

Housing in Orange: what your pipes are made of — and why it matters

Ask a plumber in Orange what matters most on a first visit and the answer is usually the same: the age of the house and the material of its pipes. The two are linked, and knowing the link tells you what is likely to fail next.

Before the 1960s: galvanised steel and cast iron

The oldest homes in Orange usually still have galvanised steel supply pipes and cast-iron drains. Inside, galvanised steel corrodes and the bore narrows year by year: pressure falls, the water runs rust-coloured after a holiday, and in time a joint weeps or a section blocks. Cast-iron drains scale and crack the same way, and tree roots find them first. A repair buys time, but a house where pressure has dropped at every tap is usually ready for a planned repipe in copper or PEX.

1960s to 1980s: copper supply, plastic drains

Homes built in the 1960s, 70s and 80s in Orange are typically copper for supply and plastic for drains. Copper holds up well, though pinhole leaks from aggressive water and ageing soldered joints are not unusual. Watch the sewer lateral: even when the house is copper, the lateral to the street is often clay or cast iron, and roots exploit it. A camera survey settles it quickly.

1990s to 2000s: copper and early PEX

The 1990s and 2000s brought the first PEX and a lot more copper, with PVC drains throughout. PEX from this period is flexible and resistant to scale, which suits Orange well, but early fittings and manifolds deserve a look: a manifold with a slow weep is easy to miss behind a panel. Water heaters from this era are now reaching the end of their working life, so a heater check is worth doing even if the hot water still runs.

2010s onwards: PEX and CPVC

Homes built since the 2010s in Orange are mostly PEX or CPVC, often run from a central manifold that lets each fixture be isolated on its own. The pipe rarely fails; trouble usually starts at a fitting, a valve or an appliance connection. Tankless heaters are common in these homes too, and they need descaling on schedule — in hard-water country, skipping it is the fastest route to a cold shower.

How to tell what you have: look at an exposed pipe near the water heater or under a sink. Galvanised steel is dull grey, magnetic and joined with threads; copper is reddish-brown with soldered joints; PEX is flexible plastic, often red for hot and blue for cold; CPVC is rigid cream-coloured plastic.

What we check on a visit in Orange: the pipe material and its condition, water pressure at more than one tap, the water heater's age and anode, and every visible joint for weeping. The report is plain: what is fine, what needs watching, and what should be planned before it fails.

Local water & water heaters

Yes. Salt in sea air speeds up corrosion on exposed brass and copper fittings, outdoor taps and water-heater connections, and damp fog keeps crawl spaces moist so slow leaks hide longer. We check exposed metalwork on every visit, and replacing a corroded fitting early is far cheaper than repairing the water damage it causes later.

A standard tank water heater typically lasts 8 to 12 years, and in Orange the local water is the deciding factor: scale shortens its life, so a heater that is flushed every year usually outlasts one that never is. Rust-coloured water, rumbling noises and falling hot-water pressure are the signs that replacement should be planned rather than left to fail.

Local problem patterns

Plumbing problems common on the California coast

The faults below are the ones Orange residents call about most. They follow from the local water, climate and housing, so they recur until the cause is dealt with — which is why each one gets a full treatment below.

  • Corroded outdoor taps and fittings — salt air eats unprotected metal, so exterior pipework, hose bibs and clips rust faster than the same parts inland.
  • Sand and grit in outdoor drains — wind-blown sand settles in gullies and yard drains, slowing them until a heavy rain backs water up.
  • Damp crawl spaces — sea air keeps the space under the house moist, which corrodes fittings and encourages mould where leaks go unnoticed.
  • Ageing supply lines in older coastal homes — many pre-1970s houses still run on galvanised pipe that narrows with corrosion and cuts water pressure.
  • Storm drains overwhelmed by heavy rain — short, sharp downpours can flood low spots when gutters and surface drains are even partly blocked.

A quick annual look at outdoor taps, drains and gutters is the simplest defence — and it is the visit most coastal homeowners skip.
