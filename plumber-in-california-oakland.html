Know your home

Housing in Oakland: what your pipes are made of — and why it matters

Ask a plumber in Oakland what matters most on a first visit and the answer is usually the same: the age of the house and the material of its pipes. The two are linked, and knowing the link tells you what is likely to fail next.

Before the 1960s: galvanised steel and cast iron

The oldest homes in Oakland usually still have galvanised steel supply pipes and cast-iron drains. Inside, galvanised steel corrodes and the bore narrows year by year: pressure falls, the water runs rust-coloured after a holiday, and in time a joint weeps or a section blocks. Cast-iron drains scale and crack the same way, and tree roots find them first. A repair buys time, but a house where pressure has dropped at every tap is usually ready for a planned repipe in copper or PEX.

1960s to 1980s: copper supply, plastic drains

Homes built in the 1960s, 70s and 80s in Oakland are typically copper for supply and plastic for drains. Copper holds up well, though pinhole leaks from aggressive water and ageing soldered joints are not unusual. Watch the sewer lateral: even when the house is copper, the lateral to the street is often clay or cast iron, and roots exploit it. A camera survey settles it quickly.

1990s to 2000s: copper and early PEX

The 1990s and 2000s brought the first PEX and a lot more copper, with PVC drains throughout. PEX from this period is flexible and resistant to scale, which suits Oakland well, but early fittings and manifolds deserve a look: a manifold with a slow weep is easy to miss behind a panel. Water heaters from this era are now reaching the end of their working life, so a heater check is worth doing even if the hot water still runs.

2010s onwards: PEX and CPVC

Homes built since the 2010s in Oakland are mostly PEX or CPVC, often run from a central manifold that lets each fixture be isolated on its own. The pipe rarely fails; trouble usually starts at a fitting, a valve or an appliance connection. Tankless heaters are common in these homes too, and they need descaling on schedule — in hard-water country, skipping it is the fastest route to a cold shower.

How to tell what you have: look at an exposed pipe near the water heater or under a sink. Galvanised steel is dull grey, magnetic and joined with threads; copper is reddish-brown with soldered joints; PEX is flexible plastic, often red for hot and blue for cold; CPVC is rigid cream-coloured plastic.

What we check on a visit in Oakland: the pipe material and its condition, water pressure at more than one tap, the water heater's age and anode, and every visible joint for weeping. The report is plain: what is fine, what needs watching, and what should be planned before it fails.

Local conditions

Water and climate in Oakland: what local conditions do to your plumbing

Fog and damp are the Bay Area's signature. Crawl spaces stay moist for much of the year, which lets slow leaks and condensation damage hide, while heavy winter rain tests drains, gutters and sump pumps — and hillside homes put extra load on drainage.

Water in most Bay Area districts is moderately hard — enough to fur up a showerhead over a few years and to make a yearly heater flush worthwhile. In older homes with galvanised pipe the effect is worse, because scale narrows pipes that corrosion has already narrowed.

None of this is a reason to worry; it is a reason to maintain. In Oakland the homes that cost their owners least are the ones where the heater is flushed yearly, the outdoor lines are checked before the hard season, and a small leak is fixed while it is still small.

The faults below are the ones Oakland residents call about most. They follow from the local water, climate and housing, so they recur until the cause is dealt with — which is why each one gets a full treatment below.

  • Tree roots in sewer laterals — mature street trees send roots into old clay and cast-iron joints, and the first sign is slow drains in several rooms at once.
  • Hillside drain runs — long laterals on sloped lots are harder to clear and more prone to sagging sections where waste collects.
  • Seismic vulnerability — rigid gas and water connections can fail in a quake, which is why flexible connectors and shut-off valves are standard precautions here.
  • Damp winters and fog — cool, moist air keeps crawl spaces damp for months, hiding slow leaks and corroding exposed fittings.
  • Discoloured water from old pipes — rust inside ageing supply lines shows up first as brown water from a tap that has stood overnight.

Start with the age of the pipes and the state of the sewer line — those two checks tell you most of what you need to know.
