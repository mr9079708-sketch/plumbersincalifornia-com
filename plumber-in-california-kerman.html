Know your home

Housing in Kerman: what your pipes are made of — and why it matters

A plumber who does not know what your pipes are made of is guessing. In Kerman, as across the region, the material depends mostly on when the house was built, and each era has its own failure pattern. This is what we see, era by era.

Before the 1960s: galvanised steel and cast iron

The oldest homes in Kerman usually have galvanised steel supply pipes and cast-iron drains. Galvanised steel corrodes from the inside, so the bore narrows year by year: pressure falls, water runs rust-coloured after a holiday, and eventually a joint weeps or a section blocks. Cast-iron drains scale and crack in the same way, and they are the pipes tree roots find first. A repair buys time; a house where the pressure has dropped in every tap is usually ready for a planned repipe in copper or PEX rather than another patch.

1960s to 1980s: copper supply, plastic drains

From the 1960s on, Kerman homes usually have copper supply lines and ABS or PVC drains. Copper is a long-lived material, but aggressive water can open pinhole leaks and soldered joints age with the house. The drains are generally sound — the weak point is the sewer lateral, the run to the street, which is often clay or cast iron even in a copper house, and that is where roots get in. A camera inspection answers the question in an hour.

1990s to 2000s: copper and early PEX

In 1990s and 2000s homes in Kerman, expect copper and early PEX with PVC drains. The PEX itself is durable and scale-resistant, but the fittings and manifolds from that period should be looked at, and water heaters installed then are now nearing replacement age — worth checking before they fail rather than after.

2010s onwards: PEX and CPVC

Newer homes in Kerman are mostly PEX or CPVC, often run from a central manifold so each fixture can be isolated on its own. The pipe itself rarely fails; when something does go wrong it is usually a fitting, a valve or an appliance connection. Tankless water heaters are common in this era too, and they need descaling on a schedule — in hard-water country, skipping it is the fastest way to a cold shower.

How to tell what you have: look at an exposed pipe near the water heater or under a sink. Galvanised steel is dull grey, magnetic and joined with threads; copper is reddish-brown with soldered joints; PEX is flexible plastic, often red for hot and blue for cold; CPVC is rigid cream-coloured plastic.

What we check on a visit in Kerman: the pipe material and its condition, water pressure at more than one tap, the water heater's age and anode, and every visible joint for weeping. The report is plain: what is fine, what needs watching, and what should be planned before it fails.

Rust-coloured water, rumbling noises and falling hot-water pressure are the signs that replacement should be planned rather than left to fail.

Local problem patterns

Plumbing problems common in the Central Valley

Local conditions write the plumbing story in Kerman. Around here, the same five faults appear year after year — the list below, and the detail on each one further down the page.

  • Dust and debris in outdoor drains — dry-season dust and field debris collect in gullies, then the first autumn rain turns them into a blockage.
  • Running toilets wasting water — a worn flapper in a Valley home can waste hundreds of litres a day, and the water bill is usually the first clue.
  • Outdoor taps left running for gardens — Valley gardens need daily watering in summer, and a tap or hose left on can flood a yard or run up the bill.
  • Appliance hookups under strain — dishwashers and washing machines run hard in large Valley households, and their supply hoses are a common leak point.
  • Scale in water heaters — hard water lays down mineral scale inside tanks and on heating elements, cutting efficiency and shortening the heater's life.

Most Valley plumbing trouble is mineral trouble. A yearly heater flush and a look at the fixtures buys years of quiet service.
