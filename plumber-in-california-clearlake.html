What your home’s age means

What Clearlake’s homes mean for your plumbing

Two houses on the same street in Clearlake can have completely different plumbing, because the year they were built decides what is in the walls. Knowing what era a home belongs to tells you what to check, what is likely to fail first, and whether a repair or a replacement is the honest answer.

Built before 1960: galvanised steel and cast iron

Before 1960, the standard was galvanised steel for supply and cast iron for drains, and both are now past their best in Clearlake. The steel rusts from the inside, narrowing the bore until pressure falls and the water runs brown; the cast iron scales and sags until drains block more often. Test the lines before anything fails — a pressure and flow test, plus a camera look at the drains, tells you what is urgent and what can wait.

1960s to 1980s: copper supply, plastic drains

The 1960s to 1980s brought copper supply lines and plastic drains, and most of that work is still sound in Clearlake. Copper's weakness is pinhole leaks where the water chemistry is aggressive, and the drains' weakness is age — joints and fittings from this era are now old enough to fail. The checks are targeted: pressure, visible joints, and the busiest bathrooms first.

1990s to today: PEX and CPVC

PEX and CPVC define homes built from the 1990s onward: flexible pipe that resists freezing and scale, and installs quickly. The weak points moved, they did not vanish — fittings, manifolds and the quality of the installation now decide whether the system is trouble-free. Exposed runs still need protection from sun, and a bad manifold will show up as pressure problems in one part of the house and not another.

Across inland California, the habits matter more than the era: flush the heater annually, replace the anode rod on schedule, descale the aerators when flow drops, and check outdoor lines before the heat peaks. Whatever the pipes are made of, those four jobs are what keep a system healthy.

Preventing clogged drains due to tree roots

02

Slab leaks under concrete floors

Why it happens: Many California homes sit on concrete slabs, and a pinhole leak in a buried hot-water line shows up as a warm patch on the floor, a faint sound of running water, or a water bill that climbs for no reason.

How we fix it: We locate the leak with acoustic and electronic detection, then reroute the line overhead or make a spot repair — whichever does less damage to the home.

Seasonal Plumbing Considerations in Clearlake

Through the year in Clearlake, the weather does most of the damage to plumbing. These are the seasonal patterns to plan around.

Spring

Spring is when winter's damage shows up: gutters full of debris, drains that slowed over the cold months, and the first heavy rain testing all of it at once.

Tip: Clear the gutters and check the drains before the spring rain arrives — a slow drain in March is a backup in April.

Summer

Summer heat can cause pipes to expand, leading to leaks and pressure issues.

Tip: Keep an eye on your water bill for unexpected increases, which may indicate leaks that need addressing.

Fall

Autumn is preparation season: gutters cleared before the rain, exposed pipes lagged before the cold, and the water heater flushed before it has to work hardest.

Tip: Book a maintenance check in the autumn — it is the cheapest visit of the year, and it prevents the expensive ones.

Winter

Winter is when small problems become emergencies: a cold snap freezes an exposed pipe, a scaled heater gives up under the extra load, and a blocked drain backs up in the first storm.

Tip: Before the cold arrives, insulate exposed pipes, clear the gutters, and have the water heater checked — the three jobs that prevent most winter call-outs.

Water and weather in Clearlake: what it does to plumbing

Plumbing fails for local reasons. In Clearlake the local reasons are hot summers, hard water and older housing stock, plus hot, dry summers and cool, wet winters — and understanding them is the difference between a repair that lasts and one that comes back next season.

Hard water drives most of the maintenance here. Scale builds inside heaters, narrows older pipes and clogs aerators, and a heater that is flushed once a year will reach its expected life while one that is never flushed dies early. The visible sign is white scale on taps; the expensive sign is a heater that gives up years before its time.

Heat works on the outside of the system. Exposed supply lines expand and contract through the summer, which loosens joints and cracks hoses and irrigation connections left in the sun; shading or lagging the exposed runs and checking them before the heat peaks prevents the split joints we see every August. Winter brings the opposite problem in short bursts: a cold snap can freeze an exposed tap or a pipe in an unheated garage, so insulating those runs before winter is cheap insurance.

The ground moves a little here too. A mix of clay and fill that shifts with moisture shift with moisture, and that seasonal movement works on buried sewer laterals and water lines — joints open, pipes belly, and roots find the gaps. A camera inspection of the lateral tells you in one visit whether the line is sound or needs work, and it is worth doing before buying an older home in Clearlake or after any backup that clears and then comes back.

Local problem patterns

Plumbing problems common across California

Across California, the same five plumbing problems turn up in town after town — including Clearlake.

  • Sewer blockages from roots and grease — the two most common causes of a main-line backup, and both are preventable with regular attention.
  • Leaks that run up the water bill — a silent toilet leak or a dripping outdoor tap can waste hundreds of litres a day.
  • Outdoor lines damaged by sun and weather — exposed pipework, hose bibs and irrigation connections wear out faster than the pipes inside the walls.
  • Washing machine hoses — rubber supply hoses age and burst without warning, and they are one of the most common causes of indoor flooding.
  • Ageing water heaters — a tank heater usually lasts around a decade, and an old unit can fail suddenly, flooding the space around it.

Watch for the early signs — slow drains, brown water, a climbing bill — and most of these stay small and cheap.
